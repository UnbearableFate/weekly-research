                                                     Multimodal LLMs are typically trained by first aligning a vision encoder with a
                                                     language model via projector training on large image-caption datasets, followed
                                                     by joint training of the full multimodal model. Despite its widespread use, the
                                                     role of the projector training stage remains poorly understood. We find that (i)
                                                     using only 10 to 20% of the data for training the projector already recovers most
                                                     downstream performance gains, and (ii) projectors trained on different datasets can
                                                     be linearly interpolated while largely preserving performance. These results suggest
                                                     that projector training mainly performs a coarse alignment, which many different
                                                     projectors achieve equally well. Motivated by these findings, we propose PORTAL,
                                                     a training-free projector initialization that requires neither paired image-caption
                                                     data nor gradient-based optimization, but only per-modality summary statistics.
                                                     PORTAL computes an optimal-transport-based map between Gaussian approxima-
                                                     tions of the vision and language feature distributions on a shared principal subspace,
                                                     so that the projector training stage can be skipped entirely. Without any paired data,
                                                     PORTAL matches the standard pipeline trained on 558k image-caption pairs across
                                                     six LLM backbones, two vision encoders, and 16 benchmarks. It outperforms that
                                                     pipeline in 3 of 7 (vision encoder, LLM) settings and reaches an average 98% of
                                                     its score across the other four.


                                         1     Introduction
                                         Modern multimodal LLMs commonly align a vision encoder with a language model by first training
                                         a projector on a large amount of image-caption paired samples, followed by joint training of the
                                         full multimodal model. This pipeline has become standard, from LLaVA [1, 2], Molmo [3], and
                                         InternVL [4] to the QwenVL series [5]. While the first stage of projector alignment has been motivated
                                         by the goal of producing a visual tokenizer compatible with the frozen LLM [1], what this stage
                                         actually does remains poorly understood. Prior works offer conflicting evidence: Prismatic [6] reports
                                         that skipping projector alignment can improve performance, whereas Cambrian-1 [7] shows that
                                         scaling the training data of projector alignment to 2.5M image-caption pairs helps. MM1 [8] suggests
                                         that factors like resolution and data mixing matter more than the projector design. These findings are
                                         difficult to reconcile without a deeper understanding of what projector alignment actually does.
                                         In this work, we show that projector alignment primarily performs coarse alignment, mapping visual
                                         features into a language-model-compatible input space which admits a large set of possible solutions.
                                         Two empirical observations support this view: (i) using only 10 to 20% of the standard 558k-sample
                                         alignment corpus achieves similar performance to training on the full dataset, and (ii) projectors
                                         trained on entirely different datasets can be linearly interpolated without losing performance. If the
                                         projector alignment learned a precise and semantically unique mapping, neither reducing the training
                                         data nor linear interpolation between independently trained projectors would preserve performance.
                                         Motivated by these observations, we propose PORTAL (Projector via Optimal-transpoRT
                                         ALignment), a training-free approach that constructs the projector from the marginal statistics

2   Related Work
Projector alignment in multimodal LLMs. Two-stage multimodal LLM training, where a vision
encoder is first aligned to a frozen language model via training a projector, followed by visual
instruction tuning, has become a standard pipeline for training multimodal LLMs. This pipeline was
popularized by LLaVA [1, 2] and replicated in InstructBLIP [9], BLIP-2 [10], Flamingo [11], Molmo
[3], InternVL [4], and the Qwen-VL series [5]. Despite its success, the role of Stage-I projector
alignment has been poorly understood and prior studies offer conflicting views. Prismatic finds
it can be skipped without a loss [6]. Cambrian-1 reports that scaling Stage-I data to 2.5M pairs
helps [7]. MM1 emphasizes resolution and data mixing over the projector itself [8]. We resolve
this disagreement by identifying what projector alignment actually does: Stage-I acts as geometric
conditioning of visual features into the LLM input distribution, not as fine-grained semantic learning.
This explains the three findings above as a single phenomenon: skipping Stage-I works when the
projector already lands in a compatible region (Prismatic [6]), more data helps only until that region
is reached (Cambrian-1 [7]), and the resolution and data mixing dominate because they affect the
conditioning more than the projector capacity does (MM1 [8]). These findings lead us to propose a
closed-form projector that requires neither paired data nor gradient updates.
Connector designs and projector geometry. Beyond the MLP used in LLaVA [1], connector
architectures span the Q-Former [10], Perceiver Resampler [11], locality-enhanced abstractors [12],
ALIGN [13], vision-expert mixtures [14], prefix-attention adapters [15], and pixel-shuffle or patch-
merging schemes for token reduction [16, 5]. A complementary line of work analyzes what these


                                                   2
connectors actually do. Verma et al. [17] track how visual information degrades through LLM layers,
Li et al. [18] probe information loss through learned projectors, Li et al. [19] report systematic norm
discrepancies between visual and text tokens, and Shukor and Cord [20] show that cross-modal
alignment emerges primarily after self-attention layers. We use the 2-layer MLP projector of LLaVA
by default, and also test PORTAL with a linear projector and with the C-Abstractor [12], which has
19× more parameters (Section 5.3).
Optimal transport for feature alignment. The Bures-Wasserstein optimal transport map between
two Gaussians has a closed form on positive-definite covariances [21, 22], which the construction
of the projector in our work exploits. Courty et al. [23] introduced OT for domain adaptation, and
Alvarez-Melis and Jaakkola [24] used Gromov-Wasserstein to align word-embedding spaces with no
paired anchors. Closest to PORTAL is CORAL [25, 26], which aligns second-order statistics with a
single linear map for domain adaptation. PORTAL extends this idea to the multimodal-alignment
setting and ties it explicitly to the Bures-Wasserstein optimum on a shared top-k principal subspace,
which we then use as a closed-form projector rather than a regularizer.
Training-free and efficient connector construction. Several recent works try to eliminate or reduce
connector training. VPGTrans [27] transfers a learned visual prompt generator across LLMs at
reduced cost. LangBridge [28] interprets visual tokens as combinations of language embeddings,
enabling cross-LLM transfer at the price of bottlenecking visual information through a vocabulary
subspace. SEMI [29] meta-learns a hypernetwork connector that adapts to new modalities from a
few samples. In the related CLIP-classification setting, training-free or lightweight adapters such as
Tip-Adapter [30] and TaskRes [31] cache or residualize over a frozen backbone. PORTAL differs
from all of the above by requiring neither paired data, gradient steps, learned caches, nor a vocabulary
bottleneck: it is a closed-form construction from per-modality summary statistics alone. Gupta et al.
[32] also learn without paired multimodal data, but to improve a unimodal representation by sharing
parameters across unpaired modalities, whereas we ask whether the projector between a frozen vision
encoder and a frozen LLM needs paired training at all.


3     What Does the Projector Alignment Actually Do?

3.1   Projector Alignment Saturates with a Small Number of Samples

To investigate the role of projector alignment, we first examine how downstream performance scales
with the amount of Stage-I training data. If strong performance requires large-scale data, it would
suggest the projector is learning fine-grained visual-language correspondences. Conversely, if a small
fraction of the data is sufficient, this would indicate that the projector primarily learns a coarse-grained
correspondence, placing visual features into a broadly compatible input space for the language model.
We conduct this analysis on the LLaVA 1.5 setup (CLIP ViT-L/14-336, Vicuna 1.5 7B, 2-layer
MLP projector), with LCS-558k for Stage-I and LLaVA-665k for Stage-II. For the data-fraction
experiment we replace Vicuna with MobileLLaMA 1.4B, which keeps 11 data fractions on two
corpora tractable. We evaluate on 16 benchmarks grouped into four categories adapted from the
Cambrian-1 [7] taxonomy: Knowledge (Know.), General (Gen.), OCR, and Vision-Centric (Vis.).
Appendix C lists the benchmarks in each. Full experimental details are in Appendix C.1.
We observe that only 10 to 20% of the LCS-558k image-caption pairs already capture most of the
downstream benefit, and all categories plateau by 30 to 40% (Figure 2). The marginal downstream
benefit of the remaining pairs is small under this protocol. ShareGPT-1.2M, more than twice as large,
does not do better: its average peaks at 38.1 with 40% of its data (Appendix D). These results suggest
that projector alignment is not primarily learning fine-grained visual-language correspondences.
Rather, it appears to perform a coarse-grained alignment step, mapping visual features into a region of
the LLM input space from which subsequent joint training can effectively adapt the full multimodal
model.

3.2   Projector Alignment Admits a Broad Solution Space

pretraining data. This observation is consistent with two possible explanations: either the set of
effective projector solutions is narrow but easy to reach, or the solution space itself is broad. To

These results clarify the role of projector alignment. The early saturation with limited data and the
traversable interpolation path between projectors trained on different data suggest that projector
alignment does not require a highly specific solution. Instead, projector alignment appears to place
visual features into a broadly compatible region of the LLM input space, where many different
projector weights can serve the same functional role.

4     PORTAL: Training-Free Projector via Optimal Transport
The previous findings suggest that projector alignment primarily serves as geometric conditioning,
placing visual features into a region of the LLM input space from which subsequent joint training can
effectively adapt the multimodal model. This mapping admits a large set of solutions, motivating a
simpler alternative to the projector alignment training stage.
We instantiate this idea with PORTAL (Projector via Optimal-transpoRT ALignment), a training-free
method that initializes the projector from the marginal feature statistics of the vision encoder and the
LLM. PORTAL estimates the mean, principal directions, and variances of each modality separately,
then constructs a closed-form optimal-transport map that aligns the source visual feature distribution
with a compatible target language-feature distribution. We also show why matching only means
and covariances can suffice. The LLM normalizes each token before attention, so attention depends
only on token directions. When token norms vary little, the spread of these directions is fixed by the
covariance, which PORTAL matches.

4.1   Closed-Form Projector Initialization

projector in standard multimodal-LLM terminology, whose output distribution is compatible with the

This construction depends only on summary statistics of each modality (i.e., means, principal

optimization. A single PCA on each side is sufficient. It is agnostic to which model produces x and
which model consumes f (x). We adopt k=512 as the default throughout the paper, which is small
enough to suppress noisy directions and large enough to preserve the dominant geometry. Varying k
over {64, 128, 256, 512, 1024} supports this choice empirically (Appendix K).
On the Gaussian assumption. Eq. (2) treats both modalities as Gaussian to obtain a closed-form
transport map in terms of means, principal directions, and the spread along each direction. Real
source and target distributions deviate from Gaussianity in tail weight and in the shape of their
marginals (Appendix H.1). We explain why the construction remains effective under these deviations
in Section 4.2.
Targeting hidden states instead of input embeddings. Eq. (2) matches visual features to the LLM’s
input embeddings, the vectors the LLM looks up for each text token before its first transformer block.
The same construction can instead target the hidden states that text tokens have after block ℓ: we
replace (µt , Vt , σ t ) by the mean, PCA basis, and spread along the principal axes of these hidden
states, collected by passing a list of English words and short phrases through the LLM. The visual
tokens still enter the LLM at its input. Only the target statistics change. Trained projectors that inject

4.2   Why Moment Matching Survives Non-Gaussianity

Real vision and text features are not Gaussian (Appendix H.1), so Eq. (2) cannot match their full
distributions. We argue that it does not need to. Every LLM we use normalizes before computing
attention logits (RMSNorm on each input token, or on queries and keys in OLMo 2), so rescaling a
token leaves its logits unchanged. Attention therefore depends only on the directions of the tokens,
and the relevant property of the projector output is how these directions are spread.
We measure this spread by the cosine dispersion ω, the standard deviation of the cosine similarity
between two tokens:

Intuitively, when all tokens have the same length, the cosine of two tokens is their dot product divided

the source value (Appendix H.3). The attention weights also depend on the learned query and key
projections, which ω does not describe.

5     Experiments

trained on 558k image-caption pairs across LLM backbones and vision encoders.

pipeline and then running the same LLaVA-665k instruction tuning for every configuration. We
compare against two baselines: (i) the standard trained LLaVA projector, obtained from 558k paired
captions, and (ii) a randomly initialized projector which skips projector alignment entirely. This setup
isolates the contribution of projector initialization: after initialization, all models receive identical
joint training.

5.1      PORTAL Matches Trained Projector Alignment Without Paired Data

We find that our closed-form projector PORTAL requiring no paired captions or gradient updates
achieves comparable downstream performance to conventional alignment pipelines trained on 558k
image-caption pairs, across a range of vision encoder and LLM combinations (Table 1). In particular,
using LLaMA 3.2 1B/CLIP and Qwen 2.5 0.5B/CLIP, PORTAL reaches 106.2% and 106.1% of the
trained baseline, respectively. Using LLaMA 3.1 8B/CLIP, it exceeds the trained baseline, reaching
102.7%. On Vicuna 1.5 with CLIP and SigLIP, Qwen 2.5 7B, and OLMo 2 1B, PORTAL remains
very close to the trained projector, reaching 98.8%, 99.3%, 98.3%, and 96.3% of the trained baseline,
respectively. Thus, PORTAL removes the requirement for paired training data and optimization cost
of projector alignment while preserving almost all of its downstream benefit after instruction tuning.
The advantage of PORTAL is clearest where the random projector fails. On SigLIP, the Random
baseline collapses to 82.5% of the trained projector. PORTAL closes that gap to 99.3%. This is
what we would expect if the alignment stage were dominated by distributional matching rather than
caption supervision.
Two settings sit below the trained baseline’s bootstrap CI, Qwen 2.5 7B/CLIP and OLMo 2 1B/CLIP,
with the gaps concentrated on OCR and Chart benchmarks. This matches the observation in Section 3.1
that OCR and Chart drive most of the Stage-I gain over the no-pretraining baseline, supporting our
reading that character-level spatial structure is the regime where paired captions teach what marginal
statistics alone cannot recover. Importantly, this phenomenon is not specific to PORTAL. Other
unpaired distribution-matching approaches, including CORAL [26] and Gromov-Wasserstein [24]
alignment, also recover most of the performance of paired Stage-I training (Appendix E.1). This


                                                                                     7
provides additional evidence that much of the benefit of Stage-I can be obtained by bringing the two
modalities into a compatible feature distribution, rather than requiring a uniquely learned image-text
correspondence.
A category-level analysis across all seven configurations further shows that this behavior is not
systematic across OCR tasks: PORTAL retains, on average, 100.7%, 102.0%, 102.4%, and 100.4%
of the trained-baseline performance on Knowledge, General, OCR, and Vision-Centric tasks, respec-
tively (Appendix E.2). We further test PORTAL beyond the standard LLaVA-665k Stage-II setting.
Under substantially larger-scale multimodal instruction tuning with 3.2M samples, PORTAL slightly
outperforms the conventionally Stage-I-trained initialization (Appendix E.3). We also evaluate object
hallucination on POPE [43] and find that PORTAL matches or exceeds the trained Stage-I baseline in
every setting (Appendix E.4).

6   Conclusion
This work rethinks projector alignment in multimodal LLMs. Our results suggest that this stage
primarily performs coarse alignment, mapping visual features into a language-model-compatible
input space rather than learning fine-grained visual-language correspondences. This view is supported
by the early saturation of alignment data, where 10 to 20% of LCS-558k recovers most downstream
gains, and by the linear interpolability of projectors trained on different datasets from a shared
initialization.
Motivated by this finding, we introduced PORTAL, a training-free projector initialization. PORTAL
uses only per-modality summary statistics, requires no paired image-caption data or gradient-based
optimization, and remains close to the 558k-trained baseline across the evaluated model pairs. As
an initialization, it reaches the trained baseline with roughly 135× lower alignment compute. These
results suggest that projector alignment can often be replaced by, or reduced to, a lightweight feature-
distribution matching step, lowering the cost and paired-data requirements for building multimodal
LLMs.
Several natural directions remain for future work. Our study focuses on projector alignment with
frozen vision and language backbones during alignment, single-image inputs, and simple projector
architectures. Extending the analysis to richer connectors, long-context grounding, multi-image
reasoning, and additional modalities such as audio, video, and 3D would further clarify how broadly
this coarse-alignment view applies. We discuss other limitations of PORTAL in Appendix A.

A     Limitations
Our analysis and method address a specific slice of the multimodal-LLM pipeline: Stage-I projector alignment
with frozen vision and language backbones. Several caveats follow from that scope. (i) The empirical analysis
in Section 3 runs on the LLaVA 1.5 architecture with a 2-layer MLP projector. Richer connectors (resamplers,
perceiver-style cross-attention, multi-stage abstractors) may not exhibit the same linear interpolability, and
we do not test them here. (ii) PORTAL is constructed from a Gaussian approximation of each modality.
The approximation captures means, principal directions, and per-axis spread, but no higher-order structure.
Appendix H.1 quantifies how far real source and target distributions deviate from this assumption. We hypothesize
that the residual gap on OCR and Chart benchmarks reflects this missing higher-order structure (character-level
spatial layout). (iii) As in LLaVA-1.5, all experiments keep the vision encoder frozen and train only the projector
and the LLM in Stage-II. We do not study end-to-end fine-tuning that also updates the vision encoder. (iv) Our
16-benchmark suite follows the Cambrian-1 grouping and inherits its biases. It does not stress long-context
grounding or multi-image reasoning, which may behave differently. (v) We instantiate PORTAL for vision-to-
language alignment. We have not tested it on audio or video encoders. The present results should be regarded as
a first existence proof rather than a finished construction. (vi) Our reading of Stage-I as geometric conditioning
is the simplest hypothesis consistent with our data. We do not claim it is the unique mechanism, and other
interpretations remain possible. (vii) The closed-form construction exceeds trained Stage-I or stays within 1.6
average points of it across the configurations we test. We make no broader claim that this holds across all
multimodal-LLM regimes. Richer benchmarks or backbones may reveal regimes where trained Stage-I genuinely
outperforms PORTAL.

BLIP captions) for Stage-I training and the standard LLaVA-665k instruction-tuning mix for Stage-II. Section 5

C.2    Cross-Seed vs. Cross-Data Interpolation
Section 3.2 reports that projectors trained on different datasets from the same initialization remain interpolable
along the linear interpolation path. This appendix complements that result with a control that varies the random
initialization instead of the training data, and contrasts the two cases.

Setup. We train two pairs of projectors using the LLaVA 1.5 setup described in Appendix C.1. Cross-data
projectors share the same random initialization but are trained on different corpora (LCS-558k and ShareGPT-
1.2M). Cross-seed projectors share the LCS-558k corpus but use two different random initializations. For
each pair (WA , WB ) we report the Frobenius distance ∥WA − WB ∥F and evaluate the linear interpolation
Wα = (1 − α) WA + α WB for α ∈ [0, 1]. Stage-II tuning is identical for every Wα .

Results. The cross-seed pair is farther apart in weight space than the cross-data pair (∥∆W ∥F = 92.4 versus
63.7), but only the cross-data pair stays inside a low-loss region under interpolation. Cross-seed CIDEr [62]
on the LLaVA captioning evaluation collapses from approximately 80 at the two endpoints to 0.7 at α = 0.5.
Cross-data projectors interpolate smoothly along the entire path and the aggregate downstream score peaks at
α = 0.5 (Figure 3).

Interpretation. Cross-seed projectors reach distinct loss basins separated by high-loss barriers, which is
the standard picture for independently initialized networks [63]. Cross-data projectors trained from the same
initialization remain inside one shared low-loss region. Initialization therefore selects the basin, and training data
selects the particular solution reached within that basin. This is the property that PORTAL targets directly: the
closed-form construction lands inside the same compatible region without using paired data.

E     Additional Experiments
In this section, we provide additional experiments that further evaluate the scope and robustness of PORTAL.
We study whether the coarse-alignment phenomenon extends to a substantially higher-capacity connector,
whether PORTAL remains effective under larger-scale multimodal instruction tuning, how its performance varies
across task categories, how it compares with alternative unpaired alignment methods, and whether training-free
alignment affects object hallucination.

E.1    Comparison with Alternative Unpaired Alignment Methods
Random initialization in our main experiments serves as a control for completely skipping Stage-I alignment
rather than as the only competing alignment method. We additionally compare PORTAL against CORAL [25],
which aligns second-order feature statistics in closed form, and Gromov-Wasserstein (GW) alignment [24], an
iterative unpaired alignment method.

F    PORTAL Construction: Pseudocode

Listing 1 states the PORTAL construction as a self-contained PyTorch routine. The function consumes a sample
of source features (e.g., CLIP patch tokens), a sample of target features (e.g., the LLM’s input embedding table
or the hidden states after block ℓ), and a rank k. It returns the parameters (W1 , b1 , W2 , b2 ) of the standard
two-layer LLaVA projector f (x) = W2 ϕ(W1 x + b1 ) + b2 from Section 4, with ϕ a GELU. The first layer
is the closed-form Gaussian optimal-transport map of Eq. (2), and the second layer is fixed to the identity. No
paired captions and no Stage-I optimization are required, only one PCA on each side.
                                                                                                                   
import torch

def portal(X, Y, k=512):
    """
    X: source features, shape (N_s, d_s) -- e.g. CLIP patch tokens.
    Y: target features, shape (N_t, d_t) -- LLM input embeddings,
                                         or post-block-l hidden
                                         states for PORTAL-ctx-l.
    k: PCA rank for the shared subspace.

    Returns (W1, b1, W2, b2) for the LLaVA projector
        f(x) = W2 @ gelu(W1 @ x + b1) + b2.
    """
    # 1. Source statistics: top-k PCA on centered source features.
    mu_s = X.mean(dim=0) # (d_s,)
    _, S_s, Vt_s = torch.linalg.svd(X - mu_s, full_matrices=False)
    V_s = Vt_s[:k].T # (d_s, k)
    sigma_s = S_s[:k] / (X.shape[0] - 1) ** 0.5 # (k,)

    # 2. Target statistics: top-k PCA on centered target features.
    mu_t = Y.mean(dim=0) # (d_t,)
    _, S_t, Vt_t = torch.linalg.svd(Y - mu_t, full_matrices=False)
    V_t = Vt_t[:k].T # (d_t, k)
    sigma_t = S_t[:k] / (Y.shape[0] - 1) ** 0.5 # (k,)

    # 3. Closed-form projector (Eq. 2): W2 fixed to identity.
    W1 = V_t @ torch.diag(sigma_t / sigma_s) @ V_s.T # (d_t, d_s)
    b1 = mu_t - W1 @ mu_s # (d_t,)
    W2 = torch.eye(Y.shape[1])
    b2 = torch.zeros(Y.shape[1])
    return W1, b1, W2, b2

Listing 1: PORTAL: training-free projector via Gaussian optimal transport. The same routine yields
PORTAL-ctxℓ when Y is replaced by post-block-ℓ hidden states (Appendix G).


Inputs. The source matrix X contains all per-patch tokens from a small unlabeled image pool: with P patches
per image and Nimg images, Ns = Nimg · P . Throughout the paper we use a default of Nimg = 1024 images
from COCO val2017 (Appendix I). For default PORTAL, the target matrix Y is the LLM’s input embedding
table, with one row per vocabulary token. For PORTAL-ctxℓ, Y instead holds the hidden states after block ℓ,
which we collect by passing a list of English words and short phrases (the concepts.txt file of LatentLens)
through the LLM (Appendix G).

Interpretation. Read right to left, V_s.T projects each centered source token onto the source’s top-k
principal axes, the diagonal rescaling diag(sigma_t / sigma_s) rescales each axis from source to target
spread, and V_t rotates the result into the target’s top-k principal frame. Adding b1 = mu_t - W1 @ mu_s
then recenters to the target mean. The composition matches first and second moments of source and target on the
k-dimensional shared subspace. We use k=512 as the default (Appendix K). The construction is well-defined
for any k ≤ min(ds , dt , Ns , Nt ).

Cost. The procedure runs two thin SVDs and two matrix multiplies. For the default setting (Ns ≈ 1024 · 576,
ds = 1024, dt = 4096, k = 512) the construction completes in under 30 seconds on a single RTX 3090
(Appendix B) and uses no captions, no paired data, and no gradient steps. This stands in contrast to standard
Stage-I projector training, which consumes a paired image-caption corpus (typically LCS-558k) and about 2,200
optimizer steps (558k pairs at batch size 256).

G      Contextualized Targets: Methodology Details
This appendix provides the implementation details behind PORTAL-ctxℓ (Section 4).

Probe corpus. We use the concepts.txt corpus released with LatentLens [64], a list of English concepts
originally assembled for probing latent directions in LLMs. The corpus offers broad semantic coverage and is
not tied to any downstream task, which makes it a representative sample of the input distribution of the LLM at
depth ℓ.

Cached hidden states. For LLMs covered by LatentLens (LLaMA 3.1 8B, Qwen 2.5 7B, Gemma 2 9B)
we use the pre-computed hidden states distributed on Hugging Face. For each LLM and each layer ℓ, the cache
stores the post-block-ℓ hidden state of every token in every concepts.txt entry, collected in a single tensor
H(ℓ) ∈ RNt ×dt . The available layers are ℓ ∈ {1, 4, 8, 12, 16, 20}. For Vicuna v1.5, which is not part of the
LatentLens release, we run concepts.txt through the model and store the corresponding hidden states in the
same format. The tensor H(ℓ) replaces the LLM’s input embedding table as the target distribution in Eq. (2).
The remainder of the PORTAL construction is unchanged.

Layer indexing. ℓ denotes the residual stream after the ℓ-th transformer block (1-indexed), following the
LatentLens convention. The setting ℓ=0 recovers standard PORTAL targeting the LLM’s input embedding table.


H      Cosine Dispersion: Theory and Empirical Checks
This section consolidates the analysis underlying Section 4.2. It quantifies how non-Gaussian the relevant
source and target distributions are (Appendix H.1), proves the spectrum-to-ω bound (Appendix H.2), reports the
empirical check of that bound on PORTAL projector outputs (Appendix H.3), and extends the empirical check
to the full MLP-GELU projector (Appendix H.4).

H.1     Distributional Audit of Source and Target Modalities
PORTAL treats both modalities as Gaussian in Eq. (2) only to obtain a closed-form transport map. The empirical
source and target distributions are not exactly Gaussian. This subsection quantifies the deviation so that the
reader can assess how reliable the second-order summary used by PORTAL is.

Procedure. For each distribution we draw n=5,000 tokens (CLIP and SigLIP patches from COCO val2017
images, rows of the LLM’s input embedding table), project the centered features onto their top k=128 principal
components and whiten the resulting coordinates to unit per-axis variance. On these coordinates we compute four
statistics. Mardia’s multivariate skewness b1 [65], reported as b1 /k3 , which is close to zero under Gaussianity.
Mardia’s kurtosis b2 [65], reported as the excess b2 /k2 − (1 + 2/k) over its Gaussian value, which quantifies tail
weight relative to a Gaussian. The fraction of the k whitened marginals for which the Anderson-Darling test [66]
rejects N (0, 1) at the 5% level. The effective dimension deff = (tr Σ)2 /tr(Σ2 ) [67], which summarizes how
variance is distributed across the leading directions. Results are reported in Table 15.

H.4        Post-Projection Statistics: Linear vs. MLP-GELU Output
The closed-form construction in Eq. (2) gives an affine map W1 x + b1 that matches the target’s first and
second moments under a Gaussian approximation. The implemented projector then passes that affine output
through GELU. This subsection reports distributional statistics of the actual projector output for each (encoder,
LLM) pair across four constructions. Random+GELU is a Random Kaiming initialization followed by GELU.
PORTAL (preact) is the PORTAL preactivation alone, which is the linear object the theory in Section 4.2 applies

projector. Trained Stage-I is a trained Stage-I projector loaded from checkpoint. The target row reports the
same statistics on the LLM’s input embedding table, restricted to the top-512 principal subspace that PORTAL

and the target mean, with a relative version normalized by ∥µt ∥. The spectrum error is the relative L2 distance
between the top-128 output and target singular values. The effective dimension is deff = (tr Σ)2 /tr(Σ2 ).

Figure 5: PCA rank sensitivity on LLaMA 3.2 1B/CLIP. Average over the 16 benchmarks as a
function of the rank k used in the PORTAL construction, with identical Stage-II tuning. All ranks
outperform the trained baseline (41.6). k=512 is optimal (44.2).

The PORTAL projector is constructed in a rank-k PCA subspace (Section 4). We vary k over
{64, 128, 256, 512, 1024} on LLaMA 3.2 1B/CLIP, constructing each projector from the same 1024 COCO
images and running identical Stage-II tuning. Figure 5 shows the resulting Avg score. Performance is flat from
k=64 to k=256 (approximately 43.3 Avg), peaks at k=512 (44.2), and decreases marginally at k=1024 (44.0).
The plateau at low ranks indicates that the dominant PCA directions already capture most of the cross-modal
structure. The peak at k=512 suggests that mid-variance directions contribute additional geometric signal. The
decrease at k=1024 is consistent with noisy eigenvectors diluting the transport map. We adopt k=512 as the
default throughout the paper.

      Question: Does the paper discuss the limitations of the work performed by the authors?
      Answer: [Yes]
      Justification: Yes, the paper discusses limitations in the Conclusion and in Appendix A.
      Guidelines:
         • The answer [N/A] means that the paper has no limitation while the answer [No] means that the
           paper has limitations, but those are not discussed in the paper.
         • The authors are encouraged to create a separate “Limitations” section in their paper.
         • The paper should point out any strong assumptions and how robust the results are to violations of
           these assumptions (e.g., independence assumptions, noiseless settings, model well-specification,
           asymptotic approximations only holding locally). The authors should reflect on how these
           assumptions might be violated in practice and what the implications would be.
         • The authors should reflect on the scope of the claims made, e.g., if the approach was only tested

           assumptions, which should be articulated.
         • The authors should reflect on the factors that influence the performance of the approach. For
           example, a facial recognition algorithm may perform poorly when image resolution is low or
           images are taken in low lighting. Or a speech-to-text system might not be used reliably to provide
           closed captions for online lectures because it fails to handle technical jargon.

4. Experimental result reproducibility
  Question: Does the paper fully disclose all the information needed to reproduce the main experimental
  results of the paper to the extent that it affects the main claims and/or conclusions of the paper
  (regardless of whether the code and data are provided or not)?
  Answer: [Yes]
  Justification: Yes, experimental details are described in the experimental setup (Section 5), and all
  hyperparameters and the evaluation protocol are given in Appendices B and C.
   Guidelines:
      • The answer [N/A] means that the paper does not include experiments.
      • If the paper includes experiments, a [No] answer to this question will not be perceived well by
        the reviewers: Making the paper reproducible is important, regardless of whether the code and
        data are provided or not.
      • If the contribution is a dataset and/or model, the authors should describe the steps taken to make
        their results reproducible or verifiable.
      • Depending on the contribution, reproducibility can be accomplished in various ways. For example,
        if the contribution is a novel architecture, describing the architecture fully might suffice, or if the
        contribution is a specific model and empirical evaluation, it may be necessary to either make it
        possible for others to replicate the model with the same dataset, or provide access to the model.
        In general. releasing code and data is often one good way to accomplish this, but reproducibility
        can also be provided via detailed instructions for how to replicate the results, access to a hosted
        model (e.g., in the case of a large language model), releasing of a model checkpoint, or other
        means that are appropriate to the research performed.
      • While NeurIPS does not require releasing code, the conference does require all submissions
        to provide some reasonable avenue for reproducibility, which may depend on the nature of the
        contribution. For example
        (a) If the contribution is primarily a new algorithm, the paper should make it clear how to
             reproduce that algorithm.
        (b) If the contribution is primarily a new model architecture, the paper should describe the
             architecture clearly and fully.
        (c) If the contribution is a new model (e.g., a large language model), then there should either be
             a way to access this model for reproducing the results or a way to reproduce the model (e.g.,
             with an open-source dataset or instructions for how to construct the dataset).
        (d) We recognize that reproducibility may be tricky in some cases, in which case authors are
             welcome to describe the particular way they provide for reproducibility. In the case of closed-
             source models, it may be that access to the model is limited in some way (e.g., to registered
             users), but it should be possible for other researchers to have some path to reproducing or
             verifying the results.

7. Experiment statistical significance
   Question: Does the paper report error bars suitably and correctly defined or other appropriate informa-
   tion about the statistical significance of the experiments?
   Answer: [Yes]
   Justification: Yes, all aggregate scores in the main results and ablation tables are reported with 95%
   confidence intervals computed from B=1,000 paired bootstrap resamples [60]; resampling is paired
   across methods within each (encoder, LLM) group so the intervals reflect method differences rather
   than sample noise. The ± subscripts in the tables denote the half-width of these CIs. The bootstrap
   protocol is described in Appendix C.
   Guidelines:
      • The answer [N/A] means that the paper does not include experiments.
      • The authors should answer [Yes] if the results are accompanied by error bars, confidence intervals,
        or statistical significance tests, at least for the experiments that support the main claims of the
        paper.
      • The factors of variability that the error bars are capturing should be clearly stated (for example,
        train/test split, initialization, random drawing of some parameter, or overall run with given
        experimental conditions).
      • The method for calculating the error bars should be explained (closed form formula, call to a
        library function, bootstrap, etc.)
      • The assumptions made should be given (e.g., Normally distributed errors).
      • It should be clear whether the error bar is the standard deviation or the standard error of the
        mean.
      • It is OK to report 1-sigma error bars, but one should state it. The authors should preferably report
        a 2-sigma error bar than state that they have a 96% CI, if the hypothesis of Normality of errors is
        not verified.
      • For asymmetric distributions, the authors should be careful not to show in tables or figures
        symmetric error bars that would yield results that are out of range (e.g., negative error rates).
      • If error bars are reported in tables or plots, the authors should explain in the text how they were
        calculated and reference the corresponding figures or tables in the text.
