                                                                                                A BSTRACT

                                                    Multi-teacher on-policy distillation (MOPD) extends on-policy distillation (OPD)
                                                    by routing domain-specific data to specialized teachers, integrating their capa-
                                                    bilities through dense token-level supervision on student-generated trajectories.
                                                    However, domain routing only distinguishes the supervision sources for different
                                                    domains; updates from all domains still act on shared parameters. When teach-
                                                    ers differ substantially in response style and domain expertise, supervision that
                                                    is effective in isolation may still lead to persistent gradient conflicts and capa-
                                                    bility degradation during joint optimization. To address this challenge, we pro-
                                                    pose Cross-Teacher Guarding for MOPD (ROAD MOPD), which introduces non-
                                                    routed teachers as auxiliary cross-domain critics on the same student trajectories
                                                    and coordinates multi-teacher supervision through token-level credit assignment
                                                    and gradient updates. Specifically, cross-teacher-guided credit assignment uses
                                                    teacher agreement to strengthen token-level corrections jointly supported by the
                                                    teachers and attenuate conflicting signals; preventive multi-teacher gradient pro-
                                                    jection uses auxiliary gradients from the same trajectories to constrain potentially
                                                    conflicting updates and adaptively controls projection strength to retain target-
                                                    domain learning signals. In experiments, ROAD MOPD mitigates degradation in
                                                    the disadvantaged domain, gains over 5 percentage points under high conflict, and
                                                    improves overall performance in low-conflict and three-domain settings.



                                         1      I NTRODUCTION

                                         Reinforcement learning (RL) has emerged as an important paradigm for post-training large language
                                         models (OpenAI, 2023; DeepSeek-AI, 2025). However, training with outcome-level rewards lacks
                                         direct feedback on intermediate reasoning steps, making it difficult to attribute a response’s success
                                         or failure to specific generation decisions. Optimization therefore remains largely dependent on
                                         policy exploration, posing challenges for credit assignment and stable optimization. On-policy dis-
                                         tillation (OPD) (Gu et al., 2024; Agarwal et al., 2024; Ko et al., 2025) offers a promising alternative
                                         for addressing this limitation: it leverages a strong teacher to provide dense token-level supervision
                                         on trajectories generated by the student’s current policy, combining on-policy sampling aligned with
                                         the student’s current generation distribution with fine-grained teacher feedback.
                                         Prior work has improved OPD capability transfer through better distillation objectives and optimiza-
                                         tion strategies (Li et al., 2026a; Lin et al., 2026; Yang et al., 2026a; Zhang et al., 2026). A natu-
                                         ral extension is to consolidate specialized teachers’ complementary capabilities in a single student
                                         across domains. Multi-teacher on-policy distillation (MOPD) (Ma et al., 2026; DeepSeek-AI et al.,
                                         2026) pursues this goal, typically through direct domain routing: a sample from domain d receives
                                         supervision only from its routed teacher Td , while updates from all domains act on the same stu-
                                         dent model. This design exploits each teacher’s domain expertise but does not explicitly coordinate
                                         updates from different domains to shared parameters. Consequently, supervision that is effective in

Prior MOPD studies have demonstrated the potential of joint learning on domain combinations such
as math and code (Yang et al., 2026b; Hou et al., 2026), but these gains may not generalize to
other combinations. As shown in Figure 1, joint training on math and code generally preserves
or improves upon the corresponding single-domain OPD performance, with relatively few gradient
conflicts. In contrast, jointly training medicine with either math or code consistently yields a high
proportion of layers with conflicting gradients, and this proportion increases further in later stages of
training. In both pairings, medical performance falls below the single-domain OPD baseline, show-
ing that the joint-learning benefits observed for math and code do not extend to these combinations.
This contrast highlights compatibility between domain updates as an important factor in MOPD
and suggests that allocating supervision through domain routing alone is insufficient to coordinate
capability transfer through shared parameters.
To address this limitation, we reconsider teachers’ roles in MOPD: can non-routed teachers help
anticipate and mitigate update interference while preserving the routed teacher’s domain-specific
supervision? We retain this supervision and introduce non-routed teachers as cross-domain critics
on the same student-generated trajectories. Their feedback on the same behavior provides local cues
about potentially incompatible learning signals. We aim to reduce joint-optimization interference
through this feedback while retaining target-domain learning signals.
Specifically, we propose Cross-Teacher Guarding for MOPD (ROAD MOPD), to exploit cross-
teacher feedback at both the token and gradient levels. At the token level, ROAD MOPD com-
pares evidence from the routed and auxiliary teachers relative to a shared frozen reference model,
strengthening supervision when their implied correction directions agree and downweighting the
corresponding tokens when they conflict. At the gradient level, auxiliary supervision on the same
trajectory defines a cross-domain reference direction, and preventive gradient projection suppresses
components of the target-domain update that conflict with this direction. In high-conflict settings,
ROAD MOPD improves Medical accuracy by 5.6–8.8 percentage points over Vanilla MOPD. It also
improves overall performance in low-conflict and three-domain settings.
Our contributions are as follows:

                                    • We identify a failure mode of domain-routed MOPD: when domain updates are poorly
                                      compatible, separately assigned supervision can induce persistent gradient conflicts in
                                      shared parameters. These conflicts accompany asymmetric performance degradation, high-
                                      lighting the importance of update compatibility for joint learning.

                                    • We propose ROAD MOPD, using non-routed teachers as cross-domain critics on the same
                                      student trajectories. Cross-teacher agreement guides token weighting, while preventive
                                      gradient projection adapts its strength under a norm-retention constraint to protect target-
                                      domain learning signals.

2     R ELATED W ORK

2.1   O N -P OLICY D ISTILLATION

Classical knowledge distillation trains a student to match a teacher’s predictive distribution (Hinton
et al., 2015). Sequence-level distillation applies this idea to autoregressive models using teacher-
generated responses, but creates a mismatch between training and inference prefixes (Kim & Rush,
2016). On-policy distillation (OPD) instead queries the teacher on student-generated trajectories,
using reverse-KL, general on-policy, or contrastive objectives (Gu et al., 2024; Agarwal et al., 2024;
Ko et al., 2025). Subsequent work analyzes sampled-token estimators (Shao et al., 2026); makes
supervision more selective through token filtering, reweighting, verifiable rewards, or reasoning
progress (Li et al., 2026a; Lin et al., 2026; Yang et al., 2026a; Zhang et al., 2026); and extends OPD
across tokenizers or intermediate generation steps (He et al., 2026; Sun et al., 2026a). Reference-
based extrapolation further scales teacher corrections against a frozen student initialization (Yang
et al., 2026b). Despite these advances, OPD can generalize unevenly across capabilities (Li et al.,
2026b). These methods refine how evidence from one assigned teacher is estimated, selected, or
scaled; they do not test its compatibility with capabilities represented by other teachers. We instead
study how domain-specialized teachers can jointly assess the same student-generated prefixes.

2.2   M ULTI -T EACHER O N -P OLICY D ISTILLATION

Multi-teacher on-policy distillation (MOPD) transfers capabilities from domain specialists to a
shared student, typically routing each rollout to its corresponding teacher for dense supervision
(Ma et al., 2026). Domain routing is also used in large-scale and staged multi-domain post-training
systems (Xiaomi LLM-Core Team et al., 2026; Yang et al., 2026c). Related methods adjust do-
main proportions during pretraining or multi-domain reinforcement learning (Xie et al., 2023; Liang
et al., 2025), but do not coordinate teacher feedback on the same trajectory. Among recent MOPD
methods, Open-MOPD balances token shares and teacher–student gaps while refreshing stale re-
wards (Gao et al., 2026), and D3 -MOPD schedules domains by their remaining distillation gaps
and learning progress (Sun et al., 2026b). Other methods separate capability-recovery and domain-
preservation updates (Chen et al., 2026), or calibrate confidence across heterogeneous teachers (Yin
et al., 2026). Gradient-conflict methods such as PCGrad and conflict-averse gradient descent instead
modify already-computed task gradients (Yu et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2021). None uses non-routed
teachers to evaluate the current target-domain trajectory. ROAD MOPD lets routed and auxiliary
teachers assess the same prefixes: their agreement guides token-level credit assignment, while aux-
iliary supervision defines preventive gradient constraints before domain updates are aggregated.

3.2      C ROSS -T EACHER -G UIDED C REDIT A SSIGNMENT

A fixed λ in Eq. (2) applies the same reward scale to every token, although a trajectory may con-
tain both transferable reasoning patterns and domain-specific corrections. The routed teacher alone
provides no cross-domain check on these corrections. We therefore query an auxiliary teacher on
the same student-generated prefixes and use its feedback to adjust the routed teacher’s token-level
reward. This strengthens supervision supported by both teachers and attenuates conflicting signals.
Importantly, the auxiliary teacher is not treated as a supervisor for solving the target-domain task.
Its relative preference is used only as a compatibility signal for the capability represented by that
teacher.

frozen reference. Their token-wise agreement is

signals are aggregated through shared parameters. We therefore formulate gradient correction as a
constrained optimization problem: minimize conflict with an auxiliary teacher on the same trajecto-
ries while preserving a minimum fraction of the target-gradient norm.

incompatible corrections while retaining target-domain learning signals. We empirically compare
these two projection strategies in Section 4.3.

Cross-teacher disagreement is associated with gradient conflict, supporting token-level credit
assignment. To examine whether cross-teacher agreement reflects update compatibility, we sample
response batches at a fixed student checkpoint, averaging max(−st , 0) (Section 3.2) over all valid
tokens per batch to obtain disagreement S− . Within each domain, we sort batches by S− from
low to high into five equal-sized groups. Figure 3 (left) plots each group’s fraction of batches
with negative cosine between raw full-parameter OPD gradients under the two teachers on identical
responses. Conflict rates trend upward from 24% to 40% in Math and 46% to 76% in Medical. This
suggests that teacher disagreement can signal potential update conflicts, providing empirical support
for agreement-guided credit assignment. Figure 3 (right) visualizes token-level credit assignment:
relative to the shared reference, opposing teacher corrections on Alternatively in Math and The in
Medical attenuate the routed teacher’s relative reward signal, whereas aligned corrections on perhaps
and Maybe in the respective domains amplify it. Probe details are provided in Appendix B.5; full
prompts and visualizations of extended responses appear in Appendix C.3 (Figure 5).
Preventive projection improves joint learning over post-hoc correction. Figure 4 compares pre-
ventive projection with PCGrad under matched training settings; both retain cross-teacher credit
assignment and the same norm-retention mechanism. PCGrad corrects conflicts between gradients
computed independently on each domain’s own trajectories. Our method instead constrains target-
domain updates using auxiliary-teacher gradients on the same rollout. This directly tests compat-
ibility between the target gradient and auxiliary-teacher feedback on the same behavior. Unlike
PCGrad’s comparison of independent domain gradients, this constraint can address local conflicts
that may persist even when those gradients align. Figure 4a shows negative mean layerwise cosines
for same-trajectory pairs during much of training, whereas independently formed domain-gradient
pairs are mostly non-negative. These patterns reflect different local compatibility criteria and do not
directly measure capability damage. Across Math × Medical and Code × Medical, ROAD MOPD
improves all four domain-average accuracies over PCGrad by 0.18–2.12 percentage points (Fig-
ure 4b). These gains support the utility of same-trajectory constraints for joint learning.
Each component improves joint performance. Table 4 shows that removing either credit assign-
ment or gradient protection lowers all four domain averages across the two domain pairs. Without
credit assignment, Math and Code accuracy drop by 1.82 and 3.48 points, respectively; removing
gradient protection reduces Medical accuracy by 0.88 points in Code × Medical. Replacing adap-
tive retention with full projection lowers the two-domain mean by 0.83 and 0.44 points in Math ×
Medical and Code × Medical, respectively, despite a 0.17-point Medical gain in the former. The full
method achieves the highest equally weighted two-domain mean in both settings. These results sup-
port adaptive retention as a way to balance conflict reduction with target-domain learning, alongside
credit assignment and gradient protection.

5   C ONCLUSION
We identify update incompatibility as a central challenge in multi-teacher on-policy distilla-
tion: domain-routed supervision can produce conflicting updates to shared student parameters.
ROAD MOPD addresses this challenge using auxiliary teachers to assess the same student tra-
jectories. Agreement-aware token weighting and preventive gradient projection suppress conflict-
ing supervision and updates while retaining target-domain learning signals. Across mathematics,
medicine, and code, ROAD MOPD mitigates high-conflict degradation, retains gains in compati-
ble settings, and achieves the best distilled-model average in each domain under joint three-domain
training. Our evaluation is limited to three text-based reasoning domains, and auxiliary scoring and
gradient computation add training cost. Extending to broader domains and larger teacher pools calls
for efficient teacher selection and compatibility estimation.

A.3   C OMPLETE T RAINING P ROCEDURE

Algorithm 1 combines teacher scoring, token modulation, and all-peer gradient projection within
one optimizer step, using the domain-mean gradient convention of the main text. All target and
auxiliary gradients are evaluated at the same student parameters on stored trajectories; only projected
target gradients enter the update. Batch normalization is detailed below, and archived projection
configurations are specified in Appendix B.3.
Token operations are elementwise over the valid responses in Bd ; stored scores and masks are re-
stricted to that domain when used. M ASKED L OSS computes the loss in Eqs. (18) and (19) using
valid response tokens. T ENSORWISE P ROJECT applies Eq. (9) separately to each parameter ten-
sor. P ROJECT P EERS S HARED F LOOR denotes the sequential multi-peer procedure detailed in Ap-
pendix A.2, subject to the shared norm floor in Eq. (24). Auxiliary gradients are discarded after
projection and never merged into gupdate .

Domain means and global-batch normalization. Equation (19) and the algorithm define each
gradient from a domain mean, and the algorithm averages the projected gradients across domains as
in Eq. (11). In the implementation path supplied with full-batch normalization metadata, masking
or slicing to domain d retains the denominator |B|. After microbatch accumulation and data-parallel
averaging, its loss contribution is therefore Lbatch
                                                d      = wd Lagrd , where wd = |Bd |/|B|; auxiliary
losses on the same responses receive the same factor. Scaling both gradients by wd > 0 preserves
the projection cosines and retention coefficients in exact arithmetic. To express this implementation
using the algorithm’s domain-mean gradients, replace its accumulation by gupdate ← gupdate +
wd ged . Balanced batches give wd = 1/K, recovering the equal-weight average in Eq. (11); shuffled
batches use their observed domain fractions. For imbalanced batches, these weights differ from 1/K
before global clipping and AdamW, so the two weighting conventions are not assumed to produce
identical optimizer steps.

B.4   BASELINE I MPLEMENTATIONS

We implement the baselines within the same MyOPD/verl training stack, using the corresponding
Qwen3-4B or Qwen3-1.7B initialization and domain teachers introduced in Section 4.1. Teacher
scoring uses the sampled tokens of student-generated responses. The baseline paths use the sampled-
token k1 reverse-KL estimator and a clipped policy-gradient surrogate with clipping parameter 0.2;
task rewards, entropy regularization, and a separate trainer KL reward are disabled. The method-
specific implementations are as follows.

Vanilla MOPD. Each prompt is routed to its domain teacher (Ma et al., 2026). The token advan-
tage log πTd (yt | ht ) − log πθ (yt | ht ) is clipped to [−10, 10] and detached, without cross-teacher
modulation or gradient projection. The archived Math–Code and Code–Medical runs each use 58
optimizer updates, while the three-domain run uses 116.

ExOPD. We use a fixed extrapolation coefficient of 1.25. The reference is the frozen student
initialization, Qwen3-4B or Qwen3-1.7B according to student size (Yang et al., 2026b). For each
sampled token, the detached advantage is 1.25(log πTd − log πref ) − (log πθ − log πref ), with all log
probabilities computed at the same response prefix. The cross-teacher gate, gradient projection, and
domain-balancing modules are disabled for this baseline.

CaMOPD. The paired-domain implementation alternates homogeneous domain batches and se-
lects examples using the rollout-time gap ∆t = log πTd (yt | ht ) − log πold (yt | ht ) (Chen et al.,
2026). Recovery examples are scored by the mean of |∆t |, while preservation examples use the
mean of max(∆t , 0). Within each branch, examples are ranked by this score and the smallest prefix
covering 80% of the total score mass is retained. For Medical–Math and Medical–Code, Medical
is the recovery branch: the update ratio is 3:1, and selected-example advantage scales are 2.0 for
Medical and 1.0 for the other domain. Math–Code uses Math as recovery and Code as preservation,
a 1:1 update ratio, and unit scales for both branches. The three-domain adaptation treats domains as
peer branches, using the positive-gap score, the same 0.8 score-mass threshold, and unit selected-
example weights. We sample Medical, Math, and Code data in a 3:1:1 ratio, prioritizing the weaker
Medical domain in line with CaMOPD’s capability-recovery objective.

Open-MOPD. We adapt its balancing and reward-refresh mechanisms to the sampled-token OPD
path (Gao et al., 2026). At rollout time, a domain’s weight is proportional to its uniform target
share divided by its observed response-token share, multiplied by its mean absolute teacher–student
gap relative to the mean gap across domains. This gap factor uses exponent 1.0 and is clipped to
[0.05, 20]. The weights are normalized to have token-weighted mean one and remain fixed within
the rollout batch. Dynamic allocation is therefore implemented through domain loss weights. Each
batch is split into four sequential optimizer minibatches. On each minibatch, the OPD advantage is
recomputed using the current student’s log probabilities and cached teacher scores, providing reward
refresh.


                                                  20
Paper Under Review




PCGrad. For the two-domain comparison (Yu et al., 2020), separate backward passes mask the
shared minibatch to each domain’s own responses. For each trainable parameter tensor, the imple-
mentation computes the two gradients’ dot product and squared norms across FSDP shards. If the
dot product is negative, both gradients are projected against the original opposing gradient; the two
corrected gradients are then summed. This symmetric two-task calculation requires no random task
ordering. Gradient snapshots use BF16 and dot products and squared norms use FP64. The compar-
ison applies global gradient clipping after merging, followed by one optimizer step. These gradients
are formed on their respective domains’ responses, following the independent-domain comparison
in Section 4.3; same-trajectory auxiliary gradients are not used for this projection. The archived
PCGrad runs whose benchmark scores appear in Table 7 also enable reward-agreement modulation
and are marked with a dagger in that table.

B.5   E VALUATION P ROTOCOLS

We evaluate 11 benchmarks across Code (HumanEval+, MBPP+, and LiveCodeBench) (Liu et al.,
2023; Jain et al., 2024), Math (AIME 2024/2025 and HMMT February/November 2025) (MAA,
2024; 2025; Harvard–MIT Mathematics Tournament, 2025a;b), and Medical (MedQA, MedX-
pertQA, MedMCQA, and SuperGPQA-Med) (Jin et al., 2020; Zuo et al., 2025; Pal et al., 2022;
M-A-P Team et al., 2025).

Generation setup. Offline evaluation uses vLLM with temperature 1.0, top-p 1.0, and a max-
imum of 16,384 generated tokens per completion. Chat prompts disable thinking mode via
enable_thinking=False.

Mathematics. Each of the four Math test sets contains 30 questions, with 32 sampled completions
per question. We append the instruction “Please reason step by step, and put your final answer within
\boxed{}.” The evaluator extracts the last boxed answer and uses math-verify to compare it
with the reference answer; a missing or malformed boxed answer is incorrect. The reported Avg@32
is the mean correctness over completions, averaged over questions:
                                             N      32

Here equality denotes equivalence under the answer verifier. Avg@32 equals the pass@1 estimate
computed from the same 32 samples. This differs from pass@32, which counts a question as solved
when at least one of its 32 completions is correct.

Code. All three Code benchmarks use four completions per problem. We report Avg@4, the
fraction of passing completions averaged over problems, which equals the pass@1 estimate from the
same four samples. HumanEval+ contains 164 problems and MBPP+ contains 378. A completion
must pass both the original and additional EvalPlus tests to count as correct. Prompts request a
Python solution in a fenced code block. Our local evaluation protocol (v3) prioritizes Python or
untagged Markdown code blocks, selects the candidate with the most nonempty lines, and uses tree-
sitter to retain the target entry point and its dependencies. Generation continues until EOS or the
token limit, without stopping at the first closing code fence. EvalPlus batches up to 256 generation
requests across problems and sets each test’s execution limit to max(5 s, 6tref ), where tref is the
reference solution’s runtime for that test.

LiveCodeBench. We use the v6 code-generation split: 175 problems with contest dates from
January 4 to April 6, 2025, giving 700 completions in total. The local loader selects test6.jsonl,
with no additional date filtering. The runner uses the Qwen3-4B non-thinking prompt template and
extracts the final fenced code block. Each completion is checked against the problem’s public and
private tests with a 60-second per-test timeout. Compilation failures, runtime errors, timeouts, and
failed tests count as incorrect completions.

Medical. The evaluation uses one completion per question and reports Avg@1 multiple-choice ac-
curacy. It covers the four-option MedQA-USMLE test set (1,273 questions), MedXpertQA Text test
set (2,450; ten options), MedMCQA validation set (4,183; four options), and the SuperGPQA sub-
set selected by discipline=Medicine (2,755; ten options). Each prompt includes the question


                                                 21
Paper Under Review




and labeled options, and requests step-by-step reasoning with the final answer letter in \boxed{}.
The parser first checks the last boxed letter, then falls back to an explicit answer phrase such as “An-
swer: B” or a terminal “(B)”. Letters are normalized to uppercase and must belong to the question’s
available options. Exact match to the reference letter determines correctness; unparseable answers
are incorrect.

Aggregation. All scores are reported as percentages. Each benchmark weights its questions
equally, and domain averages are arithmetic means over the benchmarks within that domain, ir-
respective of their different numbers of questions. The 11-task average in Table 8 weights all 11
benchmarks equally; it is distinct from averaging the three domain means, because Code contains
three benchmarks and the other domains contain four.

Teacher-disagreement and gradient-conflict probe. For the fixed-checkpoint probe in Figure 3
(left), we compute S− = meant [max(−st , 0)] over all valid response tokens pooled across each
four-trajectory batch, using Eq. (4) with τ = 1. We partition batches into five within-domain quin-
tiles, with 50 batches per quintile. A raw conflict is a negative cosine between the full-parameter
routed- and auxiliary-teacher OPD gradients on the same batch, without token modulation, projec-
tion, or optimizer updates. This five-bin analysis is exploratory; shading connects pointwise 95%
Wilson interval limits. The probe is separate from the token-visualization samples in Appendix C.3.

Gradient-norm retention during projection. Figure 9 examines when the norm constraint
changes the projection strength. For each primary domain, the logger computes two norm ratios
from the same pre-projection gradients: the ratio under full projection and the ratio under the cho-
sen adaptive strength. Each curve reports the minimum over conflicting parameter-tensor units at
that update (or one if none conflict). These are analytical projection diagnostics before merging and
global gradient clipping, rather than measurements of the final optimizer displacement.
The adaptive ratios stay at or above 0.6, within numerical precision, in all six domain traces. For
example, in the Math branch of Math × Medical, the full-projection estimate falls below 0.6 in 40 of
58 updates and reaches a minimum of 0.253, whereas the adaptive minimum is 0.600. The Medical
branch of that same run never crosses the floor, so the two curves coincide. This illustrates how the
constraint limits norm loss when needed without changing full projection when its retained norm al-
ready satisfies the floor. The full-projection curve uses the gradients encountered by ROAD MOPD;
it is not a separately trained baseline.

D    L IMITATIONS
Computational overhead. ROAD MOPD incurs additional training overhead compared with
Vanilla MOPD because of auxiliary-teacher scoring and gradient processing. Reusing student tra-
jectories avoids additional rollout generation. Future work will explore more efficient teacher eval-
uation and gradient correction to reduce this overhead.

Limited evaluation of domain scalability. Our experiments cover mathematics, code, and
medicine, with joint training evaluated on at most three domains (Table 3). Future work will as-
sess whether the observed gains and training stability extend to broader domain collections and
larger teacher pools.
