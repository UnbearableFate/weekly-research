                                              Abstract
Experience generated during deployment offers an opportunity for robots to improve policy reliability
through learning from their own executions. However, learning through human corrections requires
teleoperators to monitor execution and intervene when policies fail, a demand that is difficult to meet
as robot fleets grow. We introduce collective experience learning (CEL), a learning paradigm in which
deployed robots share their execution experience to improve a common policy. We realize CEL
through failure-guided experience retrieval fine-tuning (FERT), which uses failure contexts from robot
executions to retrieve relevant trajectory segments from the fleet's shared successful experience and
uses their actions as supervision for policy updates, without collecting additional expert
demonstrations or corrective actions. A fault-tolerant asynchronous training system (FATS)
coordinates experience collection, policy optimization and policy distribution despite local
interruptions within the robot fleet. Across five real-world manipulation tasks, the resulting policies
achieve success rates of 93.3-99.2%, evaluated over 120 trials per task on robots excluded from
training data collection. With collection time per robot held fixed, larger fleets provide more
experience and FERT generally achieves higher success rates across the evaluated sizes. On
multi-stage phone packaging, learning with 96 robots increases success from 79.2% to 98.3%. These
results support collective experience as a practical basis for scalable robot learning and a path towards
improving policy reliability during deployment without collecting additional expert corrective actions.

1   Introduction
Execution during deployment generates experience that robots can use to improve policy reliability. Robot
foundation models provide initial capabilities by translating visual and language understanding into physical
action [3, 4, 11, 20, 44], but these initial capabilities do not automatically yield reliable execution [5, 19, 31].
Variations in object properties, contact conditions and individual robots expose gaps in these capabilities,
particularly when errors accumulate over multi-stage tasks [21, 43]. At the same time, robots operating in the
real world generate experience that can support further policy improvement [1, 6]. As deployment expands
to larger fleets, parallel execution provides a growing source of physical experience [16, 17, 22, 32, 36]. The
question is how to turn this growing experience into more reliable task execution during deployment.
A central constraint is the human effort required to supply corrective actions during deployment. Expert
demonstrations and corrective interventions have proved useful for improving policies during real-world
execution [1, 13, 18, 24, 25, 34]. Recent fleet-learning systems combine parallel collection with human
supervision to connect robot execution and policy updates [14, 32, 36]. When learning depends on new expert
actions, teleoperators must remain available to monitor execution, recognize failures and take control to provide
appropriate corrections. As the deployed fleet grows, the capacity to supply this action supervision is difficult
to scale alongside robot interaction [13, 14]. This motivates a learning process in which deployed robots
improve from their own experience without relying on continued collection of expert demonstrations
or corrective actions.
We introduce Collective Experience Learning (CEL), a learning paradigm in which robots contribute
experience generated during deployment to improve a shared policy. Experience from multiple robots is
pooled for learning, and the updated policy guides subsequent fleet execution and generates further experience.
This closes the loop between distributed robot execution and shared policy improvement, allowing experience
contributed by individual robots to support learning across the fleet.
Turning collective experience into policy improvement requires selecting experience relevant to the policy’s
current difficulties. Successful trajectories differ in their relevance, so collecting more successes alone does
not determine which behaviours to emphasize during an update. We develop Failure-guided Experience
Retrieval fine-Tuning (FERT) as an algorithmic realization of CEL. FERT stores successful fleet trajectories
in a collective experience buffer and uses contexts from failed executions to retrieve relevant successful segments.
Retrieval increases the sampling priority of these segments, whose recorded actions provide supervision for
policy updates. Failures therefore guide experience selection, while successful executions supply the action
targets. Following expert-supervised initialization, FERT draws its action supervision from robot-generated
experience without collecting additional expert demonstrations or corrective actions. In our implementation,
humans label episode outcomes and reset environments, while robots supply the actions used for subsequent
learning.
Sustaining this learning loop across a robot fleet requires coordinating processes that operate at different
rates and remain subject to local interruptions. Robots may pause for environment resets, maintenance or
recovery, while experience collection, policy inference and optimization proceed on different schedules [32, 36].
We develop a Fault-tolerant Asynchronous Training System (FATS) as the system realization of CEL.
FATS coordinates robot and computing resources and decouples execution from policy optimization. Its
data plane, SwarmLink, pools cloud inference and assembles streamed trajectory fields for training. Its
control plane, HotSwarm, restarts failed robot workers in place while available robots continue collecting
experience. Whereas FERT determines how collective experience improves the shared policy, FATS supports
the distributed process through which that experience is generated, incorporated into learning and used in
subsequent execution.
We evaluate this realization of CEL across five real-world manipulation tasks involving deformable objects,
precise alignment, tool use and multi-stage execution. For each task, policies are evaluated over 120 trials, split
equally between two physical robots excluded from training data collection. This tests whether experience
contributed by the training fleet benefits other robot instances of the same platform. At the largest evaluated
fleet size for each task, the resulting policies achieve success rates of 93.3-99.2%. In fleet-size experiments
with collection time per robot held fixed, larger fleets provide more experience, and FERT success rates generally

These results show that experience generated during deployment can support further policy improvement. By
pooling experience from multiple robots to update a shared policy, CEL connects individual executions to
learning across the fleet. This offers a path towards improving robot reliability through collective operational
experience, without requiring a corresponding expansion of expert action supervision.

2     Preliminaries
2.1   Robot policies and learning from execution
We consider robots that begin operation with a policy capable of attempting the target task and use experience
generated during deployment to improve that policy. The aim is to improve task success by learning from
successful and failed executions across the fleet. Expert demonstrations and corrections can establish the
initial policy; our FERT realization of CEL uses robot-generated actions and episode-outcome feedback
without collecting further expert demonstrations or corrective actions.
Vision-language-action models combine visual observations, language instructions and action generation [3, 20].
Our implementation uses π0 , a flow-based vision-language-action model [3]. The π0 policy generates continuous
action chunks conditioned on visual observations, proprioceptive measurements and language instructions. Let
ot denote the robot observation at timestep t and g the task instruction. We write the policy as πθ (At | ot , g),
where At = (at , . . . , at+H−1 ) is an action chunk with prediction horizon H and θ denotes the policy parameters.

2.2    Problem setting
We consider policy learning during deployment for a task specified by a language instruction g, using experience
collected during robot fleet operation. Robots assigned to the same task use a shared policy, although individual
robots may execute different policy versions as learning proceeds.

measurements, at is the executed action, and T is the episode length. A binary outcome y(τ ) ∈ {0, 1} indicates

For a fixed task g, our objective is to maximize the probability of successful execution, which is the measure
of policy reliability used in our task evaluations:

Here, τ ∼ πθ denotes a trajectory generated by executing the policy in the environment. We suppress task

Experience collected across the fleet comprises execution trajectories and their associated outcomes, potentially
generated by different policy versions. The learning algorithm determines which trajectories are retained and
how they contribute to policy updates.
Starting from a task-initialized policy, CEL uses fleet-generated experience to improve the policy without
collecting additional expert demonstrations or corrective actions. Expert data may be used to establish
the initial policy. CEL specifies how fleet-generated experience is shared for continued policy improvement.
FERT is the concrete learning algorithm studied here, and FATS supplies the execution and communication
infrastructure. The task-success objective states what the policy should improve; it does not prescribe a
particular reinforcement-learning or supervised-learning update.

3     Collective Experience Learning
Collective experience learning (CEL) is a learning paradigm in which robots share experience generated
during deployment to improve a common policy. Updated policies return to the fleet for subsequent execution,
connecting experience collection and shared policy improvement in a closed loop. Figure 1 illustrates the
different sources of experience for policy learning: learning from human demonstrations, learning from expert
corrections, and learning from collective experience generated within the robot fleet. The requirement for
expert action supervision decreases from left to right.
We realize CEL through an algorithm and a training system. Following expert-supervised initialization,
failure-guided experience retrieval fine-tuning (FERT) draws action targets from successful robot trajectories
and uses failure contexts to prioritize relevant successful experience for supervised policy updates, without
collecting additional expert demonstrations or corrective actions. In this realization, expanding experience
collection does not require a corresponding expansion of expert teleoperation. Binary episode-outcome labels
distinguish successful and failed executions; humans provide these labels and reset environments, while
subsequent action supervision comes from robot executions. The fault-tolerant asynchronous training system
(FATS) coordinates robot execution, cloud inference and policy optimization across robot and computing
resources. Its asynchronous data paths and local recovery support the learning loop despite differences in
execution speed and interruptions to individual workers.

3.1   Algorithm: Failure-guided Experience Retrieval Fine-Tuning (FERT)
FERT realizes CEL by updating the policy from experience generated during deployment, without collecting
further expert demonstrations or corrective actions. It organizes autonomous execution data for retrieval and
supervised policy fine-tuning. Binary task outcomes distinguish successful executions from failures, but do
not directly specify which actions would resolve the policy’s difficulties. FERT addresses this problem by
using successful experience as a source of action targets and failure contexts as queries for selecting relevant
successful segments. Increasing the sampling priority of these segments directs learning towards behaviour
related to the policy’s current difficulties. Successes therefore supply the actions to imitate, while failures
determine where learning concentrates.

3.1.1   Step 1: Asynchronous experience collection
FERT continuously collects experience through autonomous fleet execution. Each completed trajectory τ
receives a binary task outcome y(τ ), following the setting in Section 2.2.
Successful trajectories collected across the fleet are retained in a collective experience buffer D+ , which
supplies action targets for policy fine-tuning. Failed trajectories are used as temporary retrieval queries; their
actions are not used as supervision. This separation allows FERT to use failures to guide learning without
imitating unsuccessful actions. The active buffer retains a rolling window of successful experience, bounding
the history available for training and retrieval.
Completed trajectories are received asynchronously, allowing robots to contribute experience at different rates
while policy optimization continues. Buffer insertion, retrieval and priority updates are performed between
training updates. Data balancing regulates uneven accumulation across training workers.

3.1.2   Step 2: Experience encoding
To retrieve useful behaviour across heterogeneous executions, FERT requires a representation that captures
both the scene and the robot’s configuration. We therefore combine visual features with proprioceptive state.
The visual encoder fθ̄ is a frozen copy of vision tower in the vision-language-action model, initialized from
the checkpoint at the start of this stage. Freezing the encoder keeps incoming failure queries and cached
successful trajectories in a consistent representation space as the policy is updated.

retrieve successful experience contributed by other robots through the collective experience buffer.

3.2     Fault-tolerant asynchronous training system
Learning from experience generated during deployment requires a distributed loop that connects robot
execution, experience collection and policy improvement while accommodating interruptions to individual
robots. FATS provides this system layer: each robot executes action chunks computed by cloud inference,
and its experience contributes to updates of the policy that inference serves. In our deployment this loop
spans 96 robots and six cloud servers, connected through a single 30 Gbps edge-cloud link (Appendix D). Two
properties of this setting determine how the system must be built.
The first property is that interaction and experience have different timing requirements (Table 1). A robot
needs the action chunk for its current observation before its next interaction, so delays in cloud inference or
communication slow execution. Training instead consumes complete episodes, whose records are much larger
but need not arrive immediately. Inference and experience collection share both observation data and network
capacity. The system must reuse this data and move it without making robot execution wait for training
inputs.
The second property is that physical robots have availability constraints beyond those of computing resources:
resets, battery depletion and hardware faults can interrupt their contribution to learning. In the 96-robot
run, fault incidents affected 22 robots, and some robot was interrupted every 22 min on average (Section 4.5).
Resets pause robots far more often, so the set of robots producing experience changes continuously. Most
robot faults are transient, e.g., a network interruption, a low battery or a triggered joint limit. Although
robots and accelerators are both schedulable resources, robot recovery must preserve the association with
a physical workstation and task setup. Meanwhile, the policy and optimizer state, which are expensive to
rebuild, reside in training workers. The system therefore separates recovery of experience-producing workers
from recovery of the learning state.
FATS addresses the two properties with a data plane and a control plane (Figure 3a). The data plane,
SwarmLink, connects robot execution to cloud inference and training. It pools inference requests through
distributed channels and streams trajectory fields for cloud-side assembly. The control plane, HotSwarm,
decides which stations make up the fleet, i.e., the fixed places for one robot or one accelerator, and which
worker currently occupies each of them. It detects failures, returns failed robots to their places and repairs
the data plane around them. Section 3.2.1 describes how the two planes interact, Sections 3.2.2 and 3.2.3
describe each plane, and Section 3.2.4 follows one robot fault through both.

3.2.1    Architecture
FATS separates the learning loop into three kinds of workers. Environment workers operate robots, i.e.,
they acquire observations and execute actions. Inference workers compute actions from observations using
the current policy. Training workers update the policy from collected experience. Every node describes
its accelerators and attached robots in the same way, including each robot’s communication endpoint and
cameras, and a placement specification assigns workers to these resources. In our deployment, environment
workers run at the robot site, while inference and training workers run on cloud servers (Appendix D.3).

The two planes cannot be designed separately, because every failure involves both. A crashed process leaves
data in channels and connections that the data plane must clean up, and a broken connection is often the
first sign of a failure that the control plane must handle. The planes therefore meet at three interfaces.
     • Identity flows down. HotSwarm assigns every worker an identity, and SwarmLink attaches it to the data
       that the worker produces and checks it when replies are delivered.
     • Failures flow up. A failed transfer or a lost channel replica in SwarmLink is reported to HotSwarm, in
       addition to HotSwarm’s own liveness checks.
     • Repair flows down. HotSwarm rebuilds the connections and channel replicas that a failed worker leaves
       behind, including connections that cross the edge-cloud link.




                                                                                                                  13
3.2.2   SwarmLink data plane
SwarmLink preserves independent robot progress while sharing cloud GPUs and the edge-cloud link. A robot
waits for actions answering its own observation, while training consumes completed experience from the fleet.
Distributed channels connect these two paths without imposing a common collection schedule.

Distributed channels. A channel is a named producer-consumer queue that lets workers exchange data
without fixed peer connections. Each channel has replicas on participating nodes, and each key selects a
first-in-first-out queue hosted on its first producer’s node. Producers submit locally when they use keys homed
on their own nodes, while remote consumers retrieve data directly from the home replica. Asynchronous
operations allow communication to overlap with other work.
Requests carry the robot’s station, worker identity, episode and step identifiers. Replies preserve these
identifiers and add the policy version used for inference. This associates actions with their observations and
lets HotSwarm reject stale replies after a worker restart (Section 3.2.3). HotSwarm also repairs failed replicas.
Experience held only by a failed replica may be lost, reducing the available training data without invalidating
a completed training step.

Shared inference. Cloud inference workers serve a common pool of requests rather than fixed robot groups.
A paused robot therefore leaves inference capacity available to others. Workers batch ready observations and
return each action chunk through its robot’s reply channel. Batch membership is temporary, so robots that
share an inference call can subsequently progress at different rates.
Waiting for a full batch would recreate a dependence on slower or unavailable robots. Each worker therefore
dispatches when either its batch target is reached or the oldest request reaches the batching timeout. The
target provides GPU efficiency under load, while the timeout permits smaller batches when arrivals are sparse.
This limits waiting for additional inputs, rather than time spent waiting for a busy GPU.

Trajectory streaming.   Episode data becomes available throughout execution, even though training requires
a completed record. SwarmLink separates data transfer from episode completion by assembling experience in
the cloud (Figure 3d). Inference workers contribute observations and policy outputs as requests are served.
Environment workers provide execution records, task outcomes and any additional recorded frames needed
for training. Observations already received for inference are reused rather than uploaded again as part of a
completed episode.
A trajectory collector joins these fields by worker, episode and step identifiers. It releases an episode to training
only after the required data and outcome have arrived. This preserves episode-level training semantics while
allowing communication to overlap robot execution. It also keeps trajectory ownership independent of which
inference worker serves each request.
Training-only data shares the uplink with inference requests. SwarmLink gives interaction traffic priority
and transfers bulk data in bounded chunks, allowing newly ready observations to be served between chunks.
These scheduling controls protect interaction while the collector accumulates experience. Bounded transfer
buffers apply backpressure when delivery falls behind.

3.2.3   HotSwarm control plane
HotSwarm treats the fleet as a membership that changes during training rather than a fixed set of workers. It
returns a failed robot to its own place in the fleet and keeps every other worker running, and it guarantees
that nothing left behind by the failed process is mistaken for the work of its replacement.

Stations and workers.   HotSwarm keeps a registry of stations. A station is a fixed place in a worker group,
bound to a placement, i.e., a node, its devices and, for an environment worker, one robot. Each station is
occupied by one worker, i.e., one process, at a time, and every worker receives a unique identity when it is
launched. The distinction matters because the two have different lifetimes. A station lasts for the whole
training run, because the robot and node it is bound to stay in the fleet, whereas a worker lasts only until


                                                         14
its process fails. Whatever should survive a failure, such as the robot’s place in the fleet and its reply key,
belongs to the station, and whatever must not survive it, such as messages in flight and open connections,
belongs to the worker. Membership changes through three operations on stations, namely admitting a worker
to a station, retiring it, and re-admitting a new worker to the same station. Recovering a failed robot, taking
a robot offline for maintenance and adding a robot whose node has just joined the cluster are therefore one
mechanism with three triggers. HotSwarm periodically refreshes its inventory of nodes, so a newly connected
node becomes available for placement while training runs. An environment worker spends most of its time in
a loop that exchanges observations and actions, and a message sent through its regular task queue would
wait until the loop ends. Membership changes therefore reach running workers through a separate command
channel, which a worker receives even while busy in its loop.

Tiered recovery.   The three kinds of workers differ in how often they fail and in how costly their state is to
rebuild. Environment workers are attached to physical robots and fail most often, e.g., when a robot loses
power or its network connection, but they hold almost nothing that is hard to rebuild. Their only state is the
episode in progress, which is discarded anyway when the robot is reset. Inference and training workers both
run on data-center servers, but their recovery requirements differ. Inference workers hold a replaceable copy
of the policy, whereas training workers hold the policy and optimizer state accumulated over hours of training.
A single recovery policy cannot serve both ends. Restoring the whole job from a checkpoint after every failure,
as data-center training systems commonly do, would stop the fleet every time a robot fails, whereas simply
restarting every failed process would lose the optimizer state whenever a training worker fails. HotSwarm
instead matches recovery to each kind of worker. Environment workers are restarted in place as often as
needed, because a restart costs at most one episode. Inference workers are restarted and load the current
policy before resuming service. Training workers save checkpoints periodically and resume from the latest one,
while the surviving inference workers continue serving their current policy and their experience waits in the
channels until training resumes.
HotSwarm detects failures through periodic liveness checks, failing calls and the failure reports of SwarmLink.
A process that stops responding may have crashed or may only be slow, and the two cannot be told apart
from outside. If HotSwarm waited, the station would remain in limbo, and a slow process that later resumed
could send data alongside its replacement. HotSwarm therefore terminates a suspected process instead of
waiting to learn whether it will recover. This makes every failure definite, at the cost of at most one episode
when the process was only slow.

Re-admission.      When the environment worker at a station fails, HotSwarm restores the station in five steps
(Figure 3c).
   1. It retires the failed worker. The registry keeps the retired record, marked as dead, instead of deleting it,
      so that late references to it can still be recognized as stale.
   2. It launches a new worker on the recorded placement, i.e., the same node and robot.
   3. It rebuilds the connections that the failed worker held with its peers. Peers communicate through
      connection groups that are set up once and then reused, and a failed member leaves its group unusable
      for the others. Each connection group carries an epoch, i.e., a version number that advances when the
      group is torn down, and peers rejoin only within the new epoch, so a new member never binds to an
      endpoint, i.e., a network address, published by its predecessor. Every wait during this rejoin also checks
      for peer failure and epoch advance, so it cannot block indefinitely. Transfers queued against the old
      group are replayed on the new one in their original order.
   4. It replays the station’s part of every call that was in progress when the failure occurred. Work is
      dispatched to a worker group as calls that every station executes, e.g., one rollout step on all robots.
      When one station fails during a call, only its part is executed again, by the new worker, and the results
      already returned by the other stations are kept. A single relaunch serves all such calls.
   5. The new worker resumes collection once its robot has been reset.

Worker identity across both planes.       Restoring a station means that the new worker inherits everything
the failed worker left in the system (Table 2). HotSwarm therefore attaches the worker identity to every
artifact that can outlive its process and checks it wherever the artifact is used, with a response suited to each
kind of artifact. The registry records which worker currently occupies each station, so each check is a local
comparison between the identity on the artifact and the registry entry. No coordination with other workers is
needed, which matters because the workers that would have to be consulted may themselves be failing.
The data artifacts deserve particular attention. An action computed for an observation of the failed worker
can arrive after the new worker has started. Executed by the new worker, it would move the robot according
to a state that no longer holds, and recorded in its episode, it would pair an action with an observation it was
not computed from. Neither case raises an error, and training would silently learn from inconsistent pairs.
SwarmLink prevents both by delivering an action only to the worker that sent the observation, and the new
worker discards any reply that carries its predecessor’s identity.

Decoupled progress.      Training retrieves experience from channels rather than from particular robots, and
inference serves whichever robots are waiting. A failed or paused robot therefore lowers the rate at which
experience arrives but stops nothing else, and a slow robot delays only its own episodes. In a synchronous
design, each policy update would instead wait for the slowest robot (Figure 3b). Under the fixed per-robot
fault-rate and downtime assumptions in Appendix D.4, local recovery makes both expected lost robot time
and total robot time grow in proportion to fleet size, so the expected fraction lost to faults stays constant.
A system that restarts entirely after each fault instead stops every robot at every fault. In a fleet twice as
large, faults occur twice as often and each stops twice as many robots, so the share of robot time lost roughly
doubles (Figure 3e).

3.2.4   A robot fault across both planes
The planes cooperate whenever a robot fails. Suppose the control process of a robot crashes while one of
its observations is waiting in the interaction channel and its episode is half complete. HotSwarm detects
the failure through its liveness check or through the failed delivery reported by SwarmLink, terminates any
remains of the process and retires the worker. SwarmLink continues to serve the other robots. The action
computed for the waiting observation is held under the robot’s reply key. The trajectory collector discards
the failed worker’s partial episode, including any fields already streamed to the cloud, rather than releasing it
to training. Training continues on the complete experience already queued. HotSwarm launches a new worker
at the same station, rebuilds its connections under a new epoch and replays its interrupted calls. The new
worker discards the stale action, which carries its predecessor’s identity, and its first observation starts a fresh
episode once the robot has been reset. In the 96-robot run, for example, robot 20 lost the link to the inertial
sensor of its mobile base at 11:24 and returned to collection at 11:37 after an 11-min restart in place, while
the rest of the fleet continued (Figures 13 and 15).

4       Experimental Results
We evaluate FERT and FATS as a concrete realization of CEL for learning from experience generated during
deployment. We measure whether fleet-generated experience improves task success without new expert
demonstrations or corrective actions, and how learning and its supporting system behave as more robots
contribute experience. Task success on held-out robots provides our empirical measure of reliable execution.
The controlled experiments address four questions:
      • Can fleet-generated experience improve task success beyond the initialized policy? (Section 4.2)
      • How does policy performance change with fleet size when collection time per robot is held fixed?
        (Section 4.3)
      • How do failure-guided retrieval and the other design choices affect FERT? (Section 4.4)
      • How does FATS support fleet operation in the presence of local interruptions and communication delays?
        (Section 4.5)
Section 4.1 describes the shared experimental setup. We then present policy improvement, scaling, ablation
and system results.

4.1     Experimental setup
4.1.1    Tasks and robot setup
We evaluate five real-world tabletop manipulation tasks spanning flexible and deformable objects, precise
alignment, tool use and multi-stage execution. Task durations range from tens of seconds to several minutes.
These tasks expose different sources of execution failure, from contact and alignment errors to errors that
accumulate across successive stages. Figure 4 provides an overview of the task setups. Step-by-Step explanation
of each task is available in Appendix E.

Flower Arrangement.    The robot sequentially inserts two flowers and one leaf into a vase. Flexible stems can
oscillate during motion, requiring smooth transport and accurate insertion.

Clothes Folding. The robot folds a T-shirt laid flat on a table according to a predefined procedure. The task
takes approximately 40 seconds and requires manipulation of deformable fabric and controlled contact with
the table.

Phone Charging.     At the start of each trial, the USB-C connector is already inserted into the phone. The
robot inserts a charging plug into a power strip, disconnects the Type-C cable from the phone, and places the
phone at a designated location. The task combines sequential manipulation with precise spatial alignment
and contact control during plug insertion.

Stool Assembly.   The robot places two screws at designated locations on a stool, picks up a screwdriver, and
tightens the screws. This task combines small-object manipulation and tool use; unsuccessful alignment may
require readjustment before execution can continue.

Phone Packaging.    The robot places a manual, charging cable, charger, phone case and phone into their
designated locations in a phone box, then closes the box. Execution takes approximately 2-3 minutes.
The task requires a sequence of object placements, with errors in earlier stages potentially affecting later
operations.

We use AgiBot G2 robots with a dual-arm configuration. Policy observations include one head-camera view,
two wrist-camera views and proprioceptive states. The action space consists of delta joint positions for the

4.1.2   Training setup
Our experiments use a shared expert-supervised policy initialization for FERT, RECAP and RLT. For each
task, the VLA model π0 [3] is first adapted through supervised fine-tuning (SFT), followed by human-gated
dataset aggregation (HG-DAgger) [18] across robot fleets. We refer to this implementation as multi-robot
HG-DAgger, abbreviated as (M)HG-DAgger in Figure 5: robots collect human intervention data in parallel to
train a shared task-specific policy, as detailed in Appendix C.
For each task, FERT, RECAP and RLT are initialized from the same selected (M)HG-DAgger checkpoint and
trained in separate runs. SFT and (M)HG-DAgger serve as performance references for the policy-initialization
stages. For Phone Charging, Stool Assembly and Phone Packaging, this checkpoint is obtained using mixed
multi-robot HG-DAgger, which combines data collected from the preceding HG-DAgger runs at different fleet
sizes for the same task. For Flower Arrangement and Clothes Folding, we use the checkpoint obtained from
multi-robot HG-DAgger with the largest fleet.
During subsequent FERT training, operators provide binary task-outcome labels and reset environments,
while robots autonomously generate the actions used for learning. No additional expert demonstrations or
corrective actions are collected, and the demonstration and intervention datasets used for policy initialization
are not transferred to the FERT experience buffer. Collection, optimization and policy distribution proceed
asynchronously through FATS, allowing robots to contribute experience and execute updated policy versions
during deployment. We evaluate the resulting policies on held-out robots to assess whether the collected
experience improves task success.

4.1.3   Baseline methods
We compare FERT with two approaches to learning from robot experience: single-robot online reinforcement
learning with RLT [38] and offline reinforcement learning on fleet-collected data with RECAP [1]. In our
experiments, both baselines use human corrective actions during subsequent policy learning.
RLT serves as a single-robot online reinforcement learning baseline. It learns a residual actor-critic on top
of a frozen VLA policy using sparse task rewards and human corrections collected during interaction. RLT
provides a reference for improving a policy through online reinforcement learning on an individual robot,
against which we evaluate FERT’s learning from collective fleet experience.
RECAP serves as an offline reinforcement learning baseline trained on experience collected across multiple
robots. Our implementation combines autonomous fleet rollouts with demonstrations and human corrections,
fits a value function to estimate action advantages, and fine-tunes an advantage-conditioned policy. We
use a single round of offline policy improvement on the collected dataset. RECAP therefore provides a
comparison with an alternative method that also uses fleet experience but retains expert action supervision
during subsequent policy learning.
Detailed baseline implementations, adaptations, and hyperparameters are provided in Appendix G. We
additionally report human teleoperation as a separate performance reference.

4.1.4   Evaluation protocol
We conduct evaluations on two AgiBot G2 robots excluded from training data collection. All learned-policy
evaluation trials are executed autonomously, without human intervention or corrective actions. Even robots
of the same model can exhibit instance-level variations in hardware characteristics, calibration, and execution.
We therefore use held-out robot instances to test whether policy improvements learned from collective fleet
experience transfer to robots that did not contribute experience during training. This tests transfer across
robot instances of the same platform, a requirement for applying fleet-learned policies beyond the robots that
supplied their training experience.

4.4                      Ablation studies
We study FERT’s design choices on Phone Packaging with 16 training robots (Figure 7). The full configuration
achieves a success rate of 86.7%. We examine the contributions of failure-guided retrieval, policy synchronization
frequency, retrieved-segment extension and priority retention. Table 12 reports the success counts on each
held-out test robot and the pooled success rate for every ablation configuration.

Contribution of failure-guided retrieval. We compare the full FERT configuration (w/ FR), which uses failure
contexts to retrieve relevant successful segments and increase their sampling priorities, with a no-retrieval
variant (w/o FR), which disables failure-guided retrieval and samples uniformly from the collective experience
buffer. All other settings are held fixed. Removing failure-guided retrieval reduces success from 86.7% to
64.2%, a decrease of 22.5 percentage points. This result demonstrates the benefit of selecting successful


                                                                                          22
experience based on the policy’s failure contexts, directing learning towards behaviours relevant to its current
failures. This provides empirical evidence for the experience-selection mechanism; Theorem 1 analyzes its
local effect under explicit replay and retrieval-alignment assumptions.

Effect of policy synchronization frequency.       Policy synchronization determines how quickly experience
collection incorporates updated policies. Synchronizing every 500 optimizer steps achieves the highest success
rate among the tested settings, 86.7%. Increasing the interval to 1,500 steps reduces success to 80.0%, while
shortening it to 100 steps reduces success to 75.0%. Longer intervals delay the use of updated policies, whereas
very short intervals may allow less experience to accumulate under each policy version. Within the tested
settings, these results favour a moderate synchronization interval for coupling policy refresh with experience
collection.

Effect of retrieved-segment extension.   FERT extends each retrieved interval to include neighbouring actions,
providing continuous action context before and after the matched segment (Section 3.1.3). With the maximum
query length fixed at L = 90 frames, setting hpre = hpost = 30 frames achieves 86.7% success. Removing
both extensions (hpre = hpost = 0) reduces success to 57.5%, a decrease of 29.2 percentage points. This result
highlights the importance of the actions leading into and following a matched segment for learning successful
behaviour.

4.5     System support for fleet learning
For learning to continue during deployment, FATS must keep the collective learning loop running on a fleet
whose robots fail, pause and progress at different rates, while connecting robot interaction to cloud inference
and training (Section 3.2). We examine experience-collection continuity, fault recovery and communication
in a 96-robot training run that lasted from 10:37 to 18:30. The robots paused from 12:00 to 13:00 during
the operators’ lunch break. Operators provided episode-outcome labels, monitored robot safety and reset
environments; they provided no demonstrations or corrective actions.
The evaluation addresses three questions.
   1. How do robots fail, and does HotSwarm confine each fault to the robots it affects?
   2. How does measured collection compare with trace-replay estimates for synchronous collection and for
      restarting the system after each fault?
   3. Can SwarmLink sustain cloud inference and experience collection across the fleet?
Fault and collection statistics are computed from the per-robot episode and incident intervals provided in
Appendix H.2.

4.5.2   Decoupled progress
Robots progress at different rates even without faults. The median episode lasted 3.2 min, but
per-robot medians ranged from 0.6 to 4.2 min, and robots completed between 45 and 222 episodes each
(Figure 8d). Resets and faults add pauses of varying length between episodes.
To quantify the benefits of independent collection and recovery, we replay each robot’s measured sequence
of episodes and pauses under two alternative designs. The morning and afternoon are replayed as separate
sessions, because the lunch break stops all robots at the same time of day.

Synchronous rounds.     Each policy update waits until every robot that is still collecting has finished its next
episode, and a robot starts its next episode once the round has begun and its own pause has elapsed. The
replay favors this alternative by ignoring the time to update the policy between rounds. In the trace replay,
synchronous rounds collect only 33% of the episodes of the run (Figure 8c). A round lasts a median of 9.3 min,
more than twice a robot’s typical cycle of 4.1 min, because it ends only when the robot with the longest pause
or episode finishes. Faults explain a small part of this loss. Replacing each pause that overlaps a fault with
the robot’s median pause raises synchronous collection to 42%, and replacing every pause in this way raises it
to 78%. The remaining 22% is lost because episode durations and typical pauses differ across robots and from
one episode to the next. These results show that independent collection avoids waiting caused by
uneven execution times and pauses, even in the absence of faults.

Full restart.  Every fault event stops all robots for a specified restart duration of 2, 5 or 10 min, and the
episodes in progress are discarded and repeated. The replay favors this alternative by ignoring the repair
time of the failed robot, which a full restart may also have to wait for. The full-restart replay preserves 92%,
86% and 74% of the episodes for restart durations of 2, 5 and 10 min, respectively. The analytical model
in Appendix D.4 predicts corresponding collection-time fractions of 91.6%, 81.3% and 68.5%. The replay
measures completed episodes, whereas the model estimates time available for collection; both indicate larger
losses with longer restarts. Independent recovery avoids extending these interruptions to robots
unaffected by the fault.
Under the analytical model’s fixed per-robot fault-rate and downtime assumptions, whole-system restart loses
a larger fraction of collection time as fleet size grows. Local recovery confines the recorded loss to affected
robots, 1.3% in this run, and the model predicts a fleet-size-independent expected fraction for that loss.

4.5.3   Edge-cloud communication
We profile communication during the same run. Robot interaction runs at the edge, while policy inference
and optimization run in the cloud, and the two clusters are connected through a dedicated 30 Gbps link
(Appendix D). We measure the elapsed times of send and receive operations on three channels (Figure 9).
On SwarmLink’s interaction path, Env-to-Rollout carries observations, including images and robot states, to
inference workers, and Rollout-to-Env returns action chunks for execution. Env-to-Train records experience-
transfer operations for training. These channel-operation durations do not separate network transfer from
waiting for data or computation. They characterize communication demands rather than isolating the benefits
of batching, priority scheduling or trajectory streaming.
Interaction messages are frequent and small. Three 224 × 224 RGB images contain approximately 0.45 MB
of raw uint8 image data per observation, excluding proprioceptive state and serialization overhead. Action
chunks sent through Rollout-to-Env require approximately 1.88 kB/s per robot. Episodes are larger and less
frequent. A representative Phone Packaging episode contains approximately 3,500 recorded timesteps and
occupies 1.58 GB, and robots in the run completed an episode about every 4 min.
For Env-to-Rollout, 95.18% of measured operations finish within 20 ms and more than 99% within 50 ms. For
Rollout-to-Env, 14.44% finish within 50 ms and approximately 97.6% within 250 ms. Most action-channel
operations, 83.15%, fall in the 200–250 ms interval. Although this channel offers room for optimization,
operations exceeding 1 s account for less than 1% of measurements.

5                Related Works
5.1                           Robot Learning from Real-world Experience
Real-world interaction provides experience for improving robot policies beyond their initial training. Interactive
imitation learning addresses the distribution mismatch between expert demonstrations and policy execution by
collecting expert supervision on states encountered by the learner. DAgger [34] formalizes this approach through
iterative dataset aggregation. Its human-gated variant, HG-DAgger [18], allows human experts to decide when
to take control and collects corrective behaviour during these interventions, making the interaction protocol
better suited to real-world human supervision. DART [21] addresses distribution mismatch by injecting
noise during demonstration collection, while ThriftyDAgger [13] allocates a limited intervention budget using
novelty and risk. Reinforcement learning (RL) methods also combine expert data with autonomous interaction.
HIL-SERL [25] uses demonstrations, interventions and autonomous experience for manipulation learning via
RLPD [2] algorithm backbone. ConRFT [6] incorporates human interventions into reinforcement learning
for vision-language-action policies. RECAP [1] integrates demonstrations, autonomous rollouts and expert
corrections for policy improvement. Related offline-to-online methods include AWAC [29], which combines


                                                                                                26
prior data with online interaction using advantage-weighted actor updates.
These approaches differ in how expert-provided behaviour and autonomous experience contribute to policy
updates. Our work focuses on policy improvement from experience generated by deployed robots without
collecting additional expert actions. Following expert-supervised initialization, CEL connects fleet experience
collection, shared policy updates and subsequent execution. Binary task outcomes provide evaluative feedback,
while the actions used for policy improvement come from the fleet itself.

5.2   Fleet-scale Robot Learning
Fleet-scale learning combines experience from multiple robots to improve shared policies. Ghasemipour et al.
[10] use steps-to-go predictions to construct rewards and success detectors, enabling autonomous practice
across a robot fleet with limited human supervision. SOP [32] connects fleet execution to online learning
through an asynchronous system that collects execution experience and human intervention signals and
distributes updated policies. LWD [36] connects deployment and policy improvement through fleet-scale
offline-to-online reinforcement learning using autonomous rollouts and human interventions.
These studies establish that parallel robot execution can support shared policy improvement. CEL builds
on this direction by connecting fleet-generated experience with shared policy updates during deployment,
with FERT providing the concrete learning algorithm evaluated here. We investigate this learning process
without additional expert action supervision, using training fleets of up to 96 robots. With collection time per
robot held fixed, we evaluate how policy performance changes as the training fleet and the total experience it
generates increase.

5.3   Experience Retrieval and Prioritized Learning
Retrieval and replay prioritization determine which experience receives greater emphasis during training.
Behavior Retrieval [7] uses task-specific expert data to query relevant behaviours from a larger offline dataset.
Graph Search and Retrieval [40] uses graph-based value estimates to retrieve actions for imitation. STRAP [27]
uses target demonstrations to retrieve relevant sub-trajectories from an offline dataset. Prioritized experience
replay [35] allocates updates according to temporal-difference errors, whereas self-imitation learning [30]
reinforces past actions whose returns exceed a learned value estimate. Advantage-weighted regression
(AWR) [33] fits a policy to replayed actions weighted by estimated advantages. AWAC [29] similarly uses
advantage-weighted maximum-likelihood actor updates. These methods differ in the information used to
select experience and in how the selected data influence policy updates.
FERT uses contexts from failed autonomous executions as queries into successful fleet experience. Retrieved
segments receive increased sampling priority during supervised policy updates, while failed actions are not
used as imitation targets. The retrieval queries therefore come from the fleet’s own execution rather than
target demonstrations, and the priorities do not require a learned value estimate. Successful executions supply
action targets, while failed attempts guide the allocation of updates among those targets. FERT provides a
concrete mechanism for using collective experience within the broader CEL paradigm.

5.4   Distributed Systems for Robot Learning
Distributed learning systems improve throughput by separating experience collection from policy optimization.
Ape-X [15] aggregates experience from parallel actors in a shared prioritized replay buffer, while IMPALA [8]
decouples acting and learning and uses off-policy correction to account for differences between the actors’
policies and the policy being optimized. SEED RL [9] further separates environment interaction from
centralized policy inference to improve accelerator utilization. RLlib [23] provides reusable abstractions for
composing and distributing RL computations.
In robotics, Gu et al. [12] demonstrated asynchronous off-policy learning across multiple robots, and Yahya
et al. [39] developed distributed asynchronous guided policy search to train a shared visuomotor policy.
QT-Opt [16] combined real-robot data collection with distributed replay and asynchronous optimization
for large-scale grasping. More recently, SOP [32] connected fleet execution to a centralized learner with
asynchronous policy distribution for VLA policy learning. RLinf-USER [42] provides infrastructure based on


                                                       27
Ray [28] for heterogeneous robots and cloud–edge communication, combining asynchronous learning with
persistent experience storage and crash recovery.
These studies establish asynchronous execution and shared learning infrastructure as foundations for robot
learning at scale. FATS builds on this direction to sustain CEL across a fleet, allowing policy improvement to
continue despite interruptions to individual robots. Within our approach, FERT determines how successful and
failed executions contribute to policy updates, while FATS supports the distributed process through which fleet
experience is collected and used. Together, they realize a learning loop whose subsequent action supervision
comes from robot execution, with binary outcome feedback supplied by operators in our experiments.

6   Conclusion
We studied how robots can use experience generated during deployment to improve policy reliability without
collecting additional expert demonstrations or corrective actions. As the fleet grows, keeping teleoperators
available to monitor execution and provide corrections becomes difficult; the fleet’s own experience offers an
alternative source of actions for learning. Collective experience learning (CEL) organizes this experience into a
shared learning loop after expert-supervised initialization. We realized CEL through failure-guided experience
retrieval fine-tuning (FERT), which uses successful robot actions as training targets and failed executions to
guide experience selection. The fault-tolerant asynchronous training system (FATS) connects robot execution,
cloud inference and policy optimization while accommodating local interruptions. FERT supplies the learning
mechanism, and FATS sustains the distributed process through which the fleet contributes and uses experience.
Across five real-world manipulation tasks, the resulting policies achieved success rates of 93.3-99.2% on robots
excluded from training data collection. With collection time per robot held fixed, larger fleets provided more
experience and generally improved FERT performance across the evaluated sizes. On Phone Packaging, training
with 96 robots raised success from 79.2% at initialization to 98.3%. Ablations supported the contribution of
failure-guided retrieval, and system measurements characterized collection and communication during fleet
operation. These findings support policy improvement from collective experience beyond expert-supervised
initialization, including transfer to other robot instances of the same platform.
The current study evaluates task-specific policies on five tasks using a single robot platform. Broader task
distributions, heterogeneous robot platforms and adaptation to changes over longer periods of operation are
directions for future work. More broadly, CEL connects the expansion of robot deployment with a growing
source of experience for shared learning. Through a common policy, experience generated by individual robots
can contribute to capabilities available across the fleet. This points towards a model of robot deployment
in which fleets contribute not only task execution, but also the experience from which their capabilities can
improve, without requiring expert action supervision to scale in proportion.

C     Policy Initialization
This appendix details the expert-supervised policy initialization used in our experiments. For each task, we
adapt the π0 policy through supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on human demonstrations, followed by human-gated
dataset aggregation (HG-DAgger) with human interventions collected across the robot fleet. The selected
task-specific checkpoint is then used as the shared initialization for FERT, RECAP and RLT. For FERT, this
expert-supervised stage precedes the CEL learning loop; subsequent action targets come from autonomous
fleet executions.

C.1     Supervised fine-tuning on human demonstrations

C.2     Multi-robot HG-DAgger with human interventions
We implement HG-DAgger [18] across multiple robots, aggregating human intervention data to update a
shared task-specific policy. This multi-robot implementation is labelled (M)HG-DAgger in Figure 5. Each
robot executes the current policy while a human operator monitors execution and takes over when correction
is needed. After the intervention, autonomous execution can resume. This procedure collects expert actions
in states encountered by the policy.
Each successful trajectory is stored in its entirety, together with a per-frame indicator identifying whether
each action was generated by the policy or supplied by a human operator. Only eligible expert action chunks
collected during human takeovers, hereafter referred to as intervention chunks, are used as action targets for
supervised policy updates.

C.2.3    Online Rollout and Policy Synchronization
We run multi-robot HG-DAgger on FATS for asynchronous collection and optimization during initialization.
FATS provides the shared infrastructure, while HG-DAgger determines how human interventions contribute
to learning. Environment workers execute actions and record intervention indicators, rollout workers provide
policy inference, and training workers optimize the policy using eligible intervention chunks. Collection and
optimization proceed concurrently.
Training begins once each training worker has accumulated sufficient valid data. Data balancing regulates
uneven accumulation across workers, and updated policy parameters are distributed to rollout workers every
Ksync optimizer steps through non-blocking asynchronous weight synchronization, where Ksync denotes the
synchronization interval. During human control, the environment worker skips repeated policy inference to
avoid unnecessary inference requests and facilitate teleoperation.

D.3   Worker roles and placement
Resource description.    Each node reports the hardware that workers can use. Accelerators are discovered
through their runtimes. Robots are registered explicitly, together with their communication endpoints and
camera identifiers, and their connectivity can optionally be checked before use. Resources are organized into
named groups, and a node may belong to several groups.

Placement.   A placement specification assigns environment, inference and training workers to resource groups.
Each worker is bound to its accelerators or robot and to the software environment of the node it runs on. The
same specification supports inference co-located with robot control or placed on a separate cluster.

Deployment placement.      Environment workers acquire observations and execute actions at the robot site.
Inference and training workers run in the cloud, so observation requests and action replies cross the dedicated
link. Trajectory assembly also runs in the cloud, where it combines inference data with execution records and
additional frames streamed from the edge.

stops all robots for time trestart . The mean interval of collection between fleet-level fault events is 1/(N λ).

Parameters.    We estimate the parameters from the 96-robot training run shown in Figures 13 and 15. The

Results.   For 96 robots, Equation (50) gives Asys = 91.6%, 81.3% and 68.5% for trestart = 2, 5 and 10 min,

Assumptions.     The model assumes that per-robot fault rates and downtime distributions do not depend on
fleet size, and that faults on different robots are independent apart from the grouping described above. It
favors whole-system restart in two respects. It ignores the repair time of the failed robot, which a whole-system
restart may also have to wait for, and it ignores the episodes in progress on other robots, which a whole-system
restart discards. The model considers only the recorded robot-local incidents, rather than shared failures of
cloud inference or the edge-cloud link. Environment resets affect both strategies and are excluded.

E     Task Setup and Evaluation Criteria
This appendix describes the observation preprocessing and action representation shared by SFT, (M)HG-
DAgger, FERT, RECAP and RLT, followed by the task-specific evaluation criteria. These specifications
define the common robot interface and task-success measurements used to evaluate the CEL realization and
its baselines.

E.1    Observation preprocessing and action representation
Observation preprocessing.    The policy input consists of an observation ot and a task instruction g. The
observation ot comprises three RGB images and a 16-dimensional proprioceptive state. The images come
from one head-mounted camera with resolution 400 × 640 and two wrist-mounted cameras with resolution
1056 × 1280 each (height × width). The state contains seven joint measurements per arm and two gripper
measurements.
All methods use the same geometric image transformations. Each image is cropped to a task-relevant region
according to Table 4, anisotropically resized to a square whose side length equals the crop’s shorter spatial
dimension, and finally resized to 224 × 224, without padding.

Action representation.     The VLA predicts action chunks

                             At = (at , . . . , at+H−1 ) ∈ RH×d ,   H = 30,   d = 16,                        (52)

where d is the action dimension and each at+k is a d-dimensional action containing seven joint commands
per arm and two gripper commands. Joint-position deltas within a chunk are relative to the robot’s joint
configuration at the start of that chunk. Each action dimension is normalized using training-dataset statistics,
which are also used to transform predictions back to the robot’s action space at inference time. RLT executes
a prefix of the VLA reference action chunk during its RL phase; its residual policy and execution horizon are
described in Appendix G.2.

Common evaluation rules.        A trial is successful only if the robot completes the prescribed task instruction
and satisfies all task-specific requirements below. Skipping a required step or executing the steps in the wrong
order constitutes failure. For Flower Arrangement, Phone Charging and Phone Packaging, remaining idle or
making no substantive progress for more than 8 s at any step constitutes failure; the corresponding threshold
for Stool Assembly is 25 s. These are limits on lack of progress within a step, rather than total task-duration
limits.
An empty grasp is a grasp attempt that fails to engage the target object. Where an empty grasp is explicitly
listed as a step-level failure below, a single occurrence causes immediate failure. For other grasping steps in
Flower Arrangement, Phone Charging, Stool Assembly and Phone Packaging where retries are permitted,
three consecutive empty grasps cause failure; a fourth attempt is not made.

Flower Arrangement.      The following steps must be completed in order under the common evaluation rules:
   1. The left gripper grasps the vase horizontally at its mouth. An empty grasp, an incorrect grasp position,
      displacement of the vase by more than 2 cm, or knocking it over constitutes failure.
   2. The right gripper picks up rose A and inserts it into the vase. An empty grasp, grasping multiple items,
      failing to insert the rose, or pulling it out while withdrawing the arm constitutes failure.
   3. The right gripper picks up rose B and inserts it into the vase, subject to the same grasping and insertion
      criteria as rose A.
   4. The right gripper picks up the leaf and inserts it into the vase, subject to the same grasping and insertion
      criteria as the roses.
   5. The left gripper releases the vase and returns to its resting configuration. Displacing the vase by more
      than 2 cm or knocking it over constitutes failure. Both roses and the leaf must remain inside the upright
      vase.

Clothes Folding.    The following steps must be completed in order under the common evaluation rules:
   1. Grasp the sleeve and hem corner on one side with both grippers and fold that side inward.
   2. Grasp one side of the garment with both grippers and drag it inward to the edge of the table.
   3. Grasp the sleeve and hem corner on the opposite side with both grippers and fold that side inward,
      mirroring Step 1.
   4. Grasp the bottom of the garment with the right gripper and fold it upward in half.
   5. Smooth out the garment with the right gripper.
Garment allocation. During training, each robot cycles through pink, green and blue garments, switching to
the next colour after 5–10 trials with the current colour. During evaluation, each held-out robot performs 20
consecutive trials with each colour, yielding 60 trials per robot across the three colours and 120 trials across
the two evaluation robots.

  2. The left arm grasps the upper part of screw B and seats it correctly in hole B. An empty grasp, an

  3. The left arm grasps the upper part of screw A and seats it correctly in hole A, subject to the same

  4. The right arm grasps the middle of the screwdriver. An empty grasp, an incorrect grasp position, or

     screwdriver, misalignment, or failure to tighten the screw constitutes failure. Both screws must be
     correctly seated and tightened at completion.

Phone Packaging.     The following steps must be completed in order under the common evaluation rules.
Every specified grasp, transfer and placement must be completed; empty grasps may be retried, but the third
consecutive empty grasp ends the trial.
  1. Pick up the top phone case from the stack and place it to the right of the phone box.
  2. Pick up the first manual from its rack and place it on the phone case.
  3. Pick up the first charging cable from the rack on the right and place it in the designated cable
     compartment inside the phone box.
  4. Use the left arm to pick up the first charging adapter from the rack on the left.
  5. Transfer the adapter from the left gripper to the right gripper in mid-air.
  6. Use the right arm to place the adapter upright in its designated compartment, then tip it into its final
     position.
  7. Pick up the phone case together with the manual, place both inside the box, and arrange them flat.
  8. Brace the box with the right arm while the left arm pushes the phone from the box lid into the box and
     arranges it flat.
  9. Continue bracing the box with the right arm while the left arm moves the lid clear, grasps it, and places
     it over the box at an angle.
 10. Keep the box braced with the right arm while the left arm pushes the lid into alignment and presses
     it down to close the box. All specified contents must be correctly placed and the box fully closed;
     otherwise, the trial is unsuccessful.

F     FERT Implementation Details
We initialize FERT from the selected task-specific (M)HG-DAgger checkpoint described in Appendix C.
Successful autonomous trajectories are stored in an in-memory rolling buffer, while failed trajectories provide
temporary retrieval queries. Retrieval embeddings are cached using a frozen visual encoder, and sampling
priorities are updated between policy updates. We optimize the policy with AdamW and cosine learning-rate
decay.
For a flow-based policy, evaluating action log-likelihoods in Equation (13) requires tracking density changes
along the generative flow. We instead retain the conditional flow-matching objective in Equation (43) as
a tractable surrogate for likelihood-based fitting. Denoising objectives admit variational interpretations in
terms of log-likelihood lower bounds under appropriate noise-level weighting. This motivates the use of flow
matching to fit the prioritized successful experience without explicitly evaluating action likelihoods.
A short derivation follows from expressing velocity prediction as noise prediction. Given Aα
                                                                                           i = (1−α)Ai +αϵnoise ,
define
                                      ϵ̂θ = Aα                 α
                                              i + (1 − α)vθ (Ai , α | oi , gi ).                           (53)
Then
                                       2                                                         2
                         ∥ϵ̂θ − ϵnoise ∥F = (1 − α)2 ∥vθ (Aα
                                                           i , α | oi , gi ) − (ϵnoise − Ai )∥F .                (54)
Thus, the flow-matching loss can be expressed as a noise-prediction loss with noise-level weighting. For
monotone weightings, the corresponding weighted denoising objective has a variational form:

                          Ci − Lζden (θ; Ai , oi , gi ) = Eα∼pζ , ϵnoise [ELBOθ,α (Aα
                                                                                    i | oi , gi )]
                                                                                                                 (55)
                                                     ≤ Eα∼pζ , ϵnoise [log pθ,α (Aα
                                                                                  i | oi , gi )] .

Here, ζ denotes the noise-level weighting, pζ is its induced distribution over noise levels, pθ,α is the corresponding
diffusion model’s marginal density, and Ci is independent of θ. Minimizing the weighted denoising loss therefore
maximizes an expected log-likelihood lower bound for noise-perturbed actions. FERT applies this likelihood-
related fitting principle through the base policy’s flow-matching loss on priority-sampled successful chunks.
For a detailed derivation, see Appendix A.1 of [26].

G     Baseline Details
The following sections specify the evaluated RECAP and RLT implementations. Both start from the same
selected expert-supervised checkpoint as FERT, but differ in their use of experience, human corrections and
policy updates. These details define the comparison configurations used in Section 4.2.

G.1    RECAP
RECAP [1] serves as our multi-robot offline reinforcement-learning baseline. We retain its value-based
advantage labeling and advantage-conditioned policy training, with the adaptations below. This baseline
evaluates policy improvement from collected fleet data with continued use of human corrective actions.
Observation preprocessing and action representation follow Appendix E.1.

Initialization and training data.  We initialize the policy from the same selected (M)HG-DAgger checkpoint
used by FERT and perform one round of offline policy improvement. The training set combines data
accumulated during (M)HG-DAgger with newly collected rollouts and human takeover data; the newly
collected data include successful and failed trajectories. Table 6 reports the data composition and frame-level
intervention fractions. The original method performs multiple rounds of policy improvement, whereas our
implementation uses a single round. The initial policy has undergone supervised training on demonstrations
and corrections, without prior advantage-conditioned training.

Task-specific value function.    We replace the original multitask, language-conditioned value model learned
from broad data with a separate vision-and-state value network for each task. It uses a frozen SigLIP encoder
and a lightweight Transformer head, and is trained on the collected task data without multimodal web
data. We retain distributional value prediction with two-hot targets and undiscounted Monte Carlo returns.
Nonterminal frames receive a reward of −1, successful termination receives 0, and failed termination receives
a task-specific negative penalty. Returns are divided by a scale no smaller than the maximum episode length
plus the magnitude of the terminal failure reward, keeping the targets within the value support [−1, 0].

Advantage conditioning.     We estimate advantages over the action-chunk horizon and label frames above
the dataset’s 70th advantage percentile as positive. Human-intervention frames are always labeled positive,
overriding both the threshold and indicator dropout. We replace the original natural-language advantage
indicators with dedicated positive, negative, and null tokens. Non-intervention samples use indicator dropout
with probability 0.3. The policy is fine-tuned with the conditional flow-matching objective and evaluated with
the positive indicator at guidance scale 1. Table 7 summarizes the key settings.

G.2   RLT
RLT [38] serves as our single-robot online reinforcement-learning baseline. It provides a comparison with
online policy improvement on an individual robot using task rewards and human corrections. We retain
its two-stage procedure: learning an RL-token representation from frozen VLA features, followed by online
actor–critic training. Observation preprocessing and action representation follow Appendix E.1.

Initialization and representation learning.We freeze the same selected (M)HG-DAgger π0 checkpoint used
to initialize FERT. An encoder–decoder is trained on the SFT dataset to compress the VLA’s image-prefix
features into an RL token through feature reconstruction. After this stage, the decoder is discarded and the
encoder remains frozen throughout online learning.

Residual action generation.   The online policy uses the RL token, proprioceptive state, and VLA reference
actions. Our actor predicts a residual around the VLA reference, whereas the original actor directly predicts
an action mean conditioned on that reference. The residual output layer is initialized to zero, and a soft
trust-region mapping bounds deviations from the reference actions in normalized action coordinates. Gripper
deviations are disabled. Table 8 summarizes the key settings, with task-specific overrides in Table 9. For
Phone Charging, residual corrections are enabled only for the right arm; the left arm and grippers follow the
VLA reference.

Online data and human supervision.      Experience is collected on one robot using a sparse reward of 1 at
successful termination and 0 otherwise. During training-data collection, the operator switches between VLA
execution and RLT execution; only RLT-phase transitions enter the replay buffer. Human corrections replace
the executed actions and behavior-cloning targets at intervention frames, and intervention transitions are
also stored in a demonstration buffer. Training batches draw equally from replay and demonstrations when
both are available. Operators provide success/failure labels and temporarily intervene during difficult task
segments for 5–10 trajectories, then return to outcome labeling, repeating this process as needed. Table 10
reports task-specific episode counts and intervention fractions.

Policy optimization and evaluation.      We use twin critics, delayed actor updates, and RLT’s chunk-level
backup, together with behavior-cloning regularization. The VLA and RL-token encoder remain frozen during
online learning. At evaluation time, switching between VLA and RLT execution is automatic, without operator
phase selection or corrective actions. The actor uses its mean without exploration noise, followed by the same
trust-region mapping used during training.
