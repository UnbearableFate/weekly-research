                                                       Policy Is Not Capability:
                                          Some Safety Benchmarks Measure Which Deﬁnition
                                                     of “Unsafe” You Trained On

                                                                                         Abstract

                                                  A guardrail classiﬁer is reported as if unsafe were a property of text. It is not:
                                                  it is a property of a policy, and the policy enters the model through its labels.
                                                  We exploit an accident of an industrial deployment in which the same ∼53k
                                                  prompts were labelled twice, under two operational deﬁnitions (“is this a request
                                                  for harmful content?” and “would a moderator block this?”), and used to train ten
                                                  guardrail arms sharing architecture, codebase and optimiser family, at two model
                                                  scales. First, the guardrail’s own training corpus already contained the bench-
                                                  marks: JailbreakBench-benign is 88% and OR-Bench-hard 82% present in the
                                                  training pools, two over-refusal benchmarks, the kind a guardrail reports to argue
                                                  it does not over-block. Everything below is decontaminated. Second, safety bench-
                                                  marks stratify sharply by how much of their score is decided by the label deﬁnition
                                                  rather than by the model: with architecture and optimiser ﬁxed, the range across
                                                  the ten arms is 0.99 on HarmBench-copyright and 0.043 on WildGuard-benign, a
                                                  23× spread. Third, a benchmark score can be moved across its entire range by
                                                  0.03% of the parameters. Across these arms HarmBench-copyright spans 0 to 100
                                                  and OR-Bench-hard over-refusal 3.4% to 56.4%, and the four arms whose last
                                                  supervision signal carries a given policy occupy one extreme of both (exact rank
                                                  p = 0.005, or 0.04–0.14 at worst), at unchanged detection capability. For a dis-
                                                  tilled student the teacher’s soft labels do this even when every hard label carries
                                                  the other policy, so teacher provenance is an unrecorded confound in any released
                                                  checkpoint. Fourth, validation accuracy on the one split that grades all twenty
                                                  arms tracks agreement with the annotator’s policy (ρ = +0.74), not detection
                                                  capability (ρ = +0.34). We argue that guardrail evaluations should report con-
                                                  tamination, a seed ﬂoor, and the deﬁnition they were graded against as ﬁrst-class
                                                  variables.


                                         1    Introduction
                                         Safety classiﬁers, commonly called guardrails, are trained and reported like any other classiﬁer. A
                                         paper states a macro-F1 on a held-out split, a set of catch rates on public benchmarks such as Harm-
                                         Bench [17], AdvBench [37], JailbreakBench [2] or StrongREJECT [30], an over-refusal number on
                                         OR-Bench [4] or XSTest [27], and the reader is invited to compare that row against another system’s
                                         row.
                                         This reporting convention contains a silent assumption: that the label unsafe names a property of the
                                         prompt, so that two systems scored on the same prompts are being compared on the same question.
                                         In deployment that assumption is visibly false. “Reproduce the lyrics of this song” is a policy
                                         violation for a content platform and an ordinary request for a drafting assistant. “Tell me a dirty
                                         joke” is blocked by a moderation product and answered by a coding assistant. Neither is a fact about

the string. Each is a decision, made once, by whoever wrote the labelling policy, and then absorbed
by every model trained on those labels.
Most work cannot separate the two, because a project has one labelling policy and therefore one
model family. We separated them by accident. Building a guardrail against a proprietary 24-pillar
safety taxonomy for an on-device assistant, we labelled the same prompt pool twice, a month apart,
with two pipelines judged comparably accurate at the time. They disagreed on one prompt in six, and
the disagreements were not noise: they were systematic, and traced to a difference in the question
the pipeline was answering. Pipeline A asked would a thoughtful moderator block this? Pipeline B
asked is this a request for harmful content? We then trained ten arms (mixing, re-weighting, anchor-
ing, distilling and head-calibrating across the two label families) at two model scales, and evaluated
all twenty models identically on ﬁfteen public safety benchmarks (33,137 prompts).
That grid is a measuring instrument for the evaluation protocols themselves. Our contributions are
diagnostic rather than methodological:

      1. The training corpus contains the benchmark suite (§3). We measure the overlap be-
         tween every benchmark and every pool the grid trained or validated on. It is severe and
         structured: 88% of JailbreakBench-benign and 82% of OR-Bench-hard, against 0% of OR-
         Bench-toxic and 2% of AdvBench. The worst hit are the over-refusal benchmarks, because
         the training class they resemble was built by harvesting them. Everything below is decon-
         taminated.
      2. Benchmarks differ enormously in how much of their score the label deﬁnition decides
         (§4). With architecture and optimiser ﬁxed, changing where the two label families enter
         training moves HarmBench-copyright across its entire range (0.99 of 1.0) and WildGuard-
         benign hardly at all (0.043). But a deﬁnition-unstable benchmark is also seed-unstable,
         and separating the two needs a seed ﬂoor almost nobody reports; we measure one from this
         codebase and show it leaves the middle of that ordering uninterpretable.
      3. A score can be moved across its full range at 0.03% of the parameters (§5). The policy
         is carried by whichever supervision signal is last (head calibration labels, or a distilled
         student’s teacher), not by the training mixture: the arms whose last signal is policy A
         occupy one extreme of HarmBench-copyright and of OR-Bench-hard at both scales (p =
         0.005, 0.14 at worst), while an arm whose hard labels are entirely policy A (40,327 of
         40,327 rows), fully ﬁne-tuned, reaches only 17.3 of 100 on HarmBench-copyright. The
         move leaves no trace in a data card.
      4. Validation accuracy measures policy agreement, not capability (§7). One policy-A-
         labelled split grades all twenty arms and ranks them by policy (ρ = +0.74) rather than by
         detection (ρ = +0.34). It is itself 69.3% inside the policy-A training ﬁle, an overlap we
         found only by running §3’s audit on our own validation data.

None of this says the benchmarks are bad or that the models are bad. It says that a substantial and
unreported fraction of what a guardrail evaluation reports is an answer to “whose policy did you
train on?”, a question the protocol never asks and the paper never states. §8 proposes the minimum
that would ﬁx it.

2   Setting
Task and taxonomy. The classiﬁer scores a single user prompt and emits (i) one of four coarse
classes, GENERIC _ SAFE, BOUNDARY _ SAFE, HARMFUL and COMPOUND (a benign request with an
embedded harmful sub-request), and (ii) a 24-way multi-label vector over an in-house taxonomy
grouped into Safety, Security, Privacy and Reliability pillars. Binary unsafe is HARMFUL ∪ COM -
POUND . The taxonomy is proprietary and does not map onto MLCommons categories, the ordinary
situation for a product guardrail and why an off-the-shelf guard is not usable directly (§10).

The two label families. Both pipelines label the same prompt pool (53,441 train / 5,936 val /
7,662 multilingual val). Policy A is the earlier, hand-curated pipeline, written against a moderation-
product rubric: it treats reproduction of copyrighted text, policy-refusal prompts and edgy-but-
harmless requests as blockable. Policy B is a later LLM-jury relabelling (four large instruct models,
three samples each at T =0.7, three-round binary cascade, ties routed to a separate ambiguous bucket;
cross-model agreement with the round majority 94.6%) written against a request-intent rubric: it
treats those same prompts as safe, because they are not requests for harmful content. On the 97,245
prompts carrying a comparable 4-class label from both pipelines, exact agreement is 83.6%; on
the 136,472 comparable at the binary level it is 90.1% (both measured on the 155k-row double-
labelled corpus, of which the 53,441-prompt training ﬁle is a fully double-labelled subset and the
two validation ﬁles 92% and 84%). Per-class retention shows where the deﬁnitions part company:
GENERIC _ SAFE 89.7% and HARMFUL 85.2%, but COMPOUND 78.3% and BOUNDARY _ SAFE only
71.2%, so the contested classes are by far the least stable. An internal spot-check judged B’s indi-
vidual labels slightly more often correct than A’s; we report it only as a direction, since the sample
was small (45 prompts) and its record was not retained. Neither family was validated against a
human-adjudicated reference set: none was built, and the jury’s chance-corrected agreement (Fleiss
κ = 0.78 binary, 0.70 on the contested BOUNDARY _ SAFE/GENERIC _ SAFE split, Appendix A) mea-
sures convergence of a procedure, not correctness. The point does not depend on which is cleaner:
label quality and label deﬁnition are independent axes, and only the ﬁrst is ever audited.

The ten arms. The arms differ in how policy A’s deﬁnition was, or was not, introduced: blended
into the training pool at various ratios, including 1,450 and 454 explicit policy-refusal anchors (E3,
E5; E2 and E4 carry none); as an inverse blend (E9); as every hard label (E6); through the distil-
lation teacher’s soft labels only (E8); or through a frozen-backbone head-only calibration pass that
updates 0.04M of 149.05M parameters (E1, E7, E10). They are not matched on everything, and
we do not claim they are: pools range from 8,489 to 144,744 rows, and the head-only arms are a
different procedure (frozen backbone, no seed set, checkpoint chosen on training loss rather than
validation). Architecture, codebase and optimiser family are shared; learning rate, warmup and ini-
tialisation are not. Table 3 lists each arm’s pool size, initialisation and teacher, read from the training
conﬁgs. All twenty models were scored by one harness in a single deterministic pass.

Evaluation suite. Fifteen public benchmarks, 33,137 prompts. Twelve scored by catch rate (frac-
tion of benchmark-harmful prompts ﬂagged unsafe): HarmBench standard/contextual/copyright,
AdvBench, JailbreakBench-harmful, StrongREJECT, DoNotAnswer [32], WildGuard-harmful [7],
SaladBench attack/defense/base [15], OR-Bench-toxic; three by over-refusal rate on benign
prompts: OR-Bench-hard, JailbreakBench-benign, WildGuard-benign, of which decontamination
(§3) leaves two usable.

3   First audit: the guardrail’s training corpus contains the benchmarks
A guardrail’s training corpus and its benchmark suite come from the same small pool of public safety
datasets, and this is the thing we nearly did not check. The pipeline that built our BOUNDARY _ SAFE
class harvested public over-refusal sets wholesale, and the evaluation harness then loaded the same
ﬁles.
For every string the harness scores we tested whether its normalised text appears in any training,
calibration or validation pool used anywhere in the grid (269,861 unique prompts). Exact match
after case and whitespace normalisation, so every number is a lower bound: it cannot see paraphrase,
translation, or a prompt embedded in a longer string, which is why HarmBench-contextual scores
0.0%. Figure 2(a) gives the result. JailbreakBench-benign is 88.0% contaminated (88/100) and
OR-Bench-hard 82.1% (1,083/1,319); HarmBench-copyright is 25.0%, WildGuard-harmful 14.3%,
StrongREJECT 12.1%.
The structure of that ordering is the ﬁnding. The two most contaminated benchmarks are over-refusal
benchmarks, the metric a guardrail paper reports to argue its model is safe to deploy. The mechanism
is convergence, not carelessness: the best public source of benign-but-edgy training prompts is the
benchmark built from benign-but-edgy prompts. Any team that builds a BOUNDARY _ SAFE-like
class from public data has probably done this; we have not seen that structure reported, though
contamination itself is a recognised threat to LLM evaluation [28, 21, 34], inferred for guardrails
behaviourally [35].
Contamination looks uneven across arms, but that unevenness is not a confound: own-pool expo-
sure (1.4–73.8%) rank-correlates −0.01 (149M) and −0.21 (395M) with the arm’s over-refusal
rate, and tracing the warm-start and teacher pools each arm inherits puts every arm at 81.0–82.3%
(Appendix A). We re-ran all twenty models with per-prompt predictions retained and recompute
everything below on clean subsets only, excluding any prompt in any pool used anywhere in the
grid: the union, not each arm’s own overlap, so the evaluated subset is identical across arms and
none is removed by the policy-A calibration pools alone. JailbreakBench-benign is dropped (12
clean prompts) and OR-Bench-hard survives at 236 of 1,319. The two effects we build on grow
(HarmBench-copyright 0.98 → 0.99, OR-Bench-hard 0.42 → 0.57); across the fourteen usable
benchmarks ten ranges rise or are ﬂat and four fall slightly (the largest fall is WildGuard-benign,
0.048 → 0.043), so contamination compresses the between-arm variation on the benchmarks it
touches rather than uniformly. Decontamination and label validity are entangled here, though: the
jury that built BOUNDARY _ SAFE admitted a prompt on a 3-of-4 majority, so the surviving OR-
Bench-hard prompts are 236 of the 353 it rejected, all 236 of which drew at least one actually
harmful vote. The 0.42 → 0.57 shift therefore reads as label composition as well as memorisation
and we cannot separate them (Appendix D).

to 4.0–16.0% against our 4.0% while Granite stays at 84.0%. Tail latency separates us from the
Qwen3Guard decoders (p95 16.1 against 94.0 ms) but not from Granite (15.6/16.4). Two caveats
beyond §9: the policy-loaded column (90.9 vs 56.1–72.4) mostly records that these benchmarks
encode rulings close to our policy A, this paper’s point rather than a result about detection; and
deploy size and latency are not like-for-like.

The disagreement. Read the other way, this replicates §4 between labs. On the 75 clean
HarmBench-copyright prompts Granite Guardian scores 0 and Qwen3Guard-4B scores 99 on iden-
tical inputs. Neither is broken: Granite Guardian’s shipped harm risk deﬁnition, the one our runs
invoked, asks whether a message is “harmful by common-sense” [22]; Qwen3Guard’s category list
names Copyright Violation. The benchmark measures which taxonomy the vendor wrote down.
Qwen3Guard makes the point again, in a released model: it emits three verdicts (Safe, Un-
safe and Controversial), closely analogous to the contested middle class our taxonomy calls
BOUNDARY _ SAFE [25] and the closest prior art on the mapping half, which already reports much of
it (Appendix B). Where a deployer maps that third class is a policy choice made after training, with
no retraining and no data. The vendor names both conventions, strict (Controversial counted unsafe)
and loose, prints both and declares no default; the rows we quote are loose, and under strict the 8B
model moves from 29.7% to 48.5% over-refusal, 72.4 to 86.8 policy and 91.8 to 96.7 capability. Our
own binary is the same lever and a larger one: counting BOUNDARY _ SAFE unsafe takes over-refusal
from 5.7% to 96.0% at 149M and 8.5% to 97.6% at 395M, while HarmBench-copyright is exactly
invariant and OR-Bench-hard saturates: §5’s rank test survives on both, but only copyright keeps its
margin (Table 2).

7   Fourth audit: validation accuracy tracks policy, not capability
The standard defence is the held-out split, and it fails here. One ﬁxed policy-A-labelled split, the
multilingual validation ﬁle, grades all twenty arms, including the fourteen trained on policy B, and
still ranks them by policy. It is also not clean: §3’s audit run on our own validation data ﬁnds
69.3% of it verbatim inside the policy-A training ﬁle, per-arm exposure 10.9–69.3% (Appendix A).
Exposure does not itself produce the ordering (rank correlation with VAL is −0.15 and +0.16), but
VAL is a policy-agreement number measured with leakage. A clean split existed and was not used:
the monolingual validation ﬁle is 0.2% inside the training ﬁle.
Across the twenty models (Figure 3, Table 1) validation accuracy correlates ρ = +0.74 with
the policy-loaded benchmarks (HarmBench-copyright, DoNotAnswer, SaladBench-defense) and
ρ = +0.34 with the capability-loaded ones (AdvBench, HarmBench-standard, JailbreakBench-
harmful, StrongREJECT, SaladBench-attack, WildGuard-harmful; criterion in Appendix A), hold-
ing within each scale (+0.64/+0.32 student, +0.83/+0.37 teacher); dropping each set’s most inﬂu-
ential member gives +0.53 and +0.07. At n=20 the intervals overlap, so we claim the ordering,
not the ratio. The contrast is between blocks, which is the mechanism and not a confound: VAL
tracks the route (+0.78), which tracks the policy-loaded set (+0.86) and not the capability-loaded
one (+0.13); inside either block it favours neither (Appendix A).

8    What we think should change
The benchmarks are ﬁne; what is missing are three columns, and all three are cheap. (1) Run the
overlap check and print it. An exact-match hash is an afternoon; near-duplicate matching is the
honest version. Ours changed which results we were willing to state, and we had to run it twice: the
ﬁrst pass covered the benchmarks but not our own validation split. Assume overlap until measured.
(2) Publish a seed ﬂoor. A benchmark delta is uninterpretable without one, and ours (54–71 points
on HarmBench-copyright) invalidated half of what we ﬁrst wrote down. Run three seeds of more
than one recipe, and report the spread. (3) State the operational deﬁnition, and say which split
you are quoting. A category list does not determine the decisions: both our pipelines used the same
24 pillars and disagreed on one prompt in six, and Qwen3Guard and Granite Guardian disagree
by 99 points on one benchmark (§6). What determines them is the rubric’s rulings on contested
cases (copyright reproduction, refusal-eliciting questions, ﬁction depicting harm, already-defended
prompts) plus, for any guard with a contested class, ours included, where that verdict is mapped.
A split drawn from your own labelling pipeline measures label ﬁt, not capability, and a differently-
sourced one is necessary but not sufﬁcient: ours is differently sourced for fourteen of twenty arms
and still ranks by policy.
Guardrail evaluation needs those three columns more than it needs new benchmarks: prior exposure,
the seed ﬂoor, and the deﬁnition a model was graded against.

9    Limitations
Variance is the main weakness: each arm is a single seed, and the seed ﬂoor of §4 comes from
neighbouring recipes rather than the arms themselves, in a direction we cannot test, so the middle
of Figure 2(b) is unresolved; we rest §5 on a rank statistic and on the two separations in level
that clear it. The head-calibration arms were trained with no seed set and selected on training loss
(Appendix A). Scope: twenty ModernBERT encoders, one proprietary 24-pillar taxonomy, two
rubrics (a two-point sample) differing in annotation modality as well as in rubric, so the speciﬁc
ordering may not transfer, though the qualitative claim should and §6 reproduces it with neither
of our pipelines involved. The arms are not matched on pool size (8,489–144,744) or checkpoint-
selection rule, and at 395M pool size and route are collinear. The overlap audit is a lower bound
(§3). Metric conventions: a benchmark-harmful prompt counts as caught if we ﬂag it, so catch
rate measures agreement with that benchmark’s rubric, which is the phenomenon under study and
why our numbers are not comparable to published ones; our binary also counts BOUNDARY _ SAFE
safe, worth 90 points of over-refusal (§6). HarmBench-copyright is two templates: its 75 clean
prompts are 39 generate the lyrics of X and 36 generate a long passage from X, so its 0.99 range is
one contested ruling read at n=75, not a broad construct. The SOTA comparison is asymmetric:
our arms were developed against these benchmarks, the baselines were not, each system is scored
under its own verdict convention, and decontamination is deﬁned by our pools only. Neither baseline
vendor publishes a train–test overlap check for these benchmarks, so their own exposure is unknown
to us and the comparison is one-sided in a direction we cannot bound, which is itself an instance of
what recommendation (1) asks releases to report. Taxonomy: pillar macro-F1 enters none of our
claims. We do not ﬁx generalisation: pooled at its deployment threshold the shipped model runs at
precision 0.968 / recall 0.496.

10    Related work
Guardrails. LlamaGuard [12], ShieldGemma [36], WildGuard [7] and Aegis [5] established the
ﬁne-tuned-classiﬁer-as-guardrail pattern; all ship a ﬁxed taxonomy, and LlamaGuard’s custom-
category parameter is cosmetic in independent reports [19], which we did not re-run. Closest to
our concern, the Granite Guardian team reports that a deﬁnitional distinction, between using and
mentioning harmful content, was the main source of error in early versions of its social-bias detec-
tor, and encodes it through taxonomy-driven synthetic data [18]; we isolate such a choice by training
on two labellings of the same prompts. Annotator disagreement and the cost of aggregating it away
are well established [23, 24, 31], including for toxicity [29, 6]; we add its behaviour under training,
as a separable component installed by the ﬁnal supervision signal. Construct validity is rarely es-
tablished [26, 13, 1, 16]: for guardrails the assumption that unsafe is annotator-independent is load-
bearing, false and never stated, and we give a quantitative instance. What transfers: distillation


                                                  8
carries bias and calibration [8, 20, 9] and other behavioural traits [3]; we add the teacher’s policy,
overriding the student’s own hard labels. That small parameter subsets steer behaviour underlies
adapters and LoRA, and head-only calibration is last-layer retraining on frozen features [10, 11, 14].

B    Prior art on the contested middle class, and on contamination
Qwen3Guard [25] is the closest prior work to §6. Its §3.4.1 reports that labelling Controversial sam-
ples unsafe better matches Aegis while labelling them safe better matches OpenAIModeration; its
§3.4.2 (Table 8, Qwen3Guard-Gen-4B only) ablates the class; and the captions of its results tables
declare a per-benchmark optimal mode. Its §3.3 also reports a training-time effect: relative to a
1:1 Safe-to-Unsafe ratio, doubling the proportion of Safe examples makes the model more permis-
sive, and it uses two such re-weighted models’ disagreement to construct the Controversial label.
Two things separate that from our claim. First, there the lever is the class ratio inside one ﬁxed
taxonomy, used as label-construction machinery, and its effect is never measured on public safety


                                                     17
benchmarks; here the lever is the deﬁnition, and we measure it across ﬁfteen decontaminated bench-
marks and twenty models. Second, its distillation stage is motivated as annotation-noise reduction
and makes no policy-transfer claim, whereas we ﬁnd distillation carries the teacher’s policy over
contrary hard labels. On contamination, the closest guardrail-speciﬁc work is Young [35], which
evaluates ten released guards on 1,445 prompts across 21 attack categories and ﬁnds every one de-
grades on novel attacks, the largest gap being Qwen3Guard-8B’s fall from 91.0% to 33.8% and the
smallest Granite-Guardian-3.2-5B’s 6.5 points; that inference is behavioural, where §3 measures cor-
pus overlap directly. On taxonomy adaptation, given a 25-category in-house taxonomy at inference
LlamaGuard3-8B scores 3.5% F1 against 84.9% on HarmBench [19].

Instrument. Every one of the 20,456 prompts harvested for the BOUNDARY _ SAFE class was la-

two Qwen-family) at temperature 0, into {BOUNDARY _ SAFE, GENERIC _ SAFE, HARMFUL, AM -

no human adjudication and no inter-rater statistic beyond the vote counts, so it is our instrument and
not ground truth.
